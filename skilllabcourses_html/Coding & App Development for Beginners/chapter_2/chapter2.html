Chapter 2: Getting Started with Scratch (Block-Based Coding)

 

Section 1: Introduction to Scratch Interface and Features

What’s Scratch All About?

Scratch is like the ultimate starter kit for coding, created by the smart folks at MIT (a super cool tech school). It’s a free, online tool where you can make interactive stories, animations, and games by snapping together blocks—no typing, no complicated stuff, just pure creativity! It’s perfect for high schoolers like you because it lets you focus on the ideas behind coding (like logic and problem-solving) without worrying about spelling errors or weird symbols. Over 70 million people worldwide use Scratch, and there are millions of projects to explore—everything from hilarious memes to epic adventure games. You’re joining a huge, creative community!

Why Start with Scratch?

Think of Scratch as training wheels for coding. It helps you understand how coding works in a visual, hands-on way before you dive into text-based languages like Python or JavaScript later in this course. Plus, it’s insanely fun—you can make a character do a backflip or say something goofy in just a few clicks. Scratch also teaches you big coding concepts (like loops and if-statements) that are used in every programming language, so you’re building real skills while having a blast. And hey, if you’ve ever dreamed of making a viral game or animation, Scratch is where tons of teens start!

Getting to Know the Scratch Interface

Let’s take a tour of Scratch so you feel right at home. Head over to on your browser (no download needed!), click “Join Scratch” to make a free account (use a cool username!), and then click “Create” to start a new project. Here’s what you’ll see on your screen:

  • Stage (Top Right): This is like your project’s TV screen—it’s where your creations come to life. Anything you code will play out here, like a character moving or speaking. It’s 480x360 pixels (kinda like a mini YouTube video frame).
  • Sprite List (Bottom Right): Sprites are your characters or objects (like a cat, ball, or spaceship). This area shows all the sprites in your project. Scratch starts you with a cute cat named “Sprite1,” but you can add more from their library or draw your own.
  • Costumes Tab (Next to Sprite List): Think of this as your sprite’s wardrobe. You can change how a sprite looks—like making the cat wear sunglasses or turn into a zombie. Each “costume” is a different look for the same sprite.
  • Blocks Palette (Left Side): This is your toolbox! It’s full of colorful blocks you drag and drop to code. Categories like “Motion” (move stuff), “Looks” (change appearance), “Sound” (play music), and “Events” (make things happen) are here. Each block does something specific, like “move 10 steps” or “say ‘Hi!’ for 2 seconds.”
  • Coding Area (Middle): This is where the magic happens. Drag blocks from the palette and snap them together here to tell your sprite what to do. It’s like building a puzzle—blocks only fit if they make sense together.
  • Green Flag &amp; Stop Buttons (Above Stage): The green flag starts your project (like hitting “play” on a video), and the red stop sign pauses it. Use these to test what you’ve made as you go.

Cool Features to Explore

Scratch is packed with stuff to make your projects pop. Here are a few features that’ll get you excited:

  • Sprite Library: Click the “Choose a Sprite” button (little cat icon below the stage) to pick from hundreds of characters—robots, dinosaurs, even pizza slices! You can also upload your own pics (like a selfie or meme) to make custom sprites.
  • Backdrop Library: Click the “Choose a Backdrop” button (landscape icon) to set a background for your stage—like a space galaxy, basketball court, or haunted house. It sets the vibe for your story or game.
  • Sound Effects: Add music or funny noises (like a “boing!” or rap beat) from the “Sounds” tab. You can even record your own voice—imagine your sprite rapping your latest TikTok sound!
  • Paint Editor: Draw your own sprites or edit existing ones with Scratch’s built-in drawing tool. Make a stick figure of your bestie or doodle a weird monster—it’s all up to you.

Teen Tip: Make It Yours!

Spend 5 minutes exploring the interface. Click random blocks, drag them around, and see what happens when you press the green flag. Don’t worry about “breaking” anything—Scratch is all about experimenting! For extra fun, pick a sprite and backdrop that match your vibe (like a skatepark with a cool skater sprite if you’re into skating). Feeling stuck? Scratch has built-in tutorials (click “Tutorials” at the top) with quick videos—perfect for visual learners like most teens.

Section 2: Creating Your First Project: Simple Animations

Let’s Make Something Move!

Now that you know your way around Scratch, let’s create your first project—an animation! Animations are like mini-movies where characters move, talk, or change looks. We’ll make the default Scratch Cat do a little dance and say something funny. This will teach you how to use blocks and test your ideas. Ready? Let’s go!

Step-by-Step: Dancing Cat Animation with Coding Steps

  • Start a New Project: Go to , log in, and click “Create.” You’ll see the Scratch Cat (Sprite1) on a blank stage.
  • Make the Cat Move: In the Blocks Palette, click the “Motion” category (blue blocks). Drag the “move 10 steps” block to the Coding Area. Click the block directly to test it—see the cat slide forward on the stage? Change “10” to “50” by typing in the box for a bigger move. Drag it back with “move -50 steps” (negative numbers move left).
  • Start with a Trigger: Click the “Events” category (yellow blocks). Drag the “when green flag clicked” block to the Coding Area and snap it above your “move” blocks. This tells Scratch to run your code when you hit the green flag (like saying “Action!” on a movie set).
  • Add a Dance Loop: Click “Control” (orange blocks) and grab the “repeat 10” block. Wrap it around your “move 50 steps” and “move -50 steps” blocks by dragging them inside the loop’s “C” shape. This repeats the back-and-forth 10 times, like a dance! Test it by clicking the green flag—your cat should shimmy!
  • Make It Talk: Go to “Looks” (purple blocks) and drag “say ‘Hello!’ for 2 seconds.” Snap it under your loop. Change “Hello!” to something funny like “I’m a dance machine!” Test it—the cat should talk after dancing.
  • Add Flair with Costumes: Click the “Costumes” tab for the cat. You’ll see different poses (like “cat-a” and “cat-b”). Back in “Looks,” grab the “switch costume to cat-b” block and add it after “move 50 steps.” Add “switch costume to cat-a” after “move -50 steps.” Now your cat changes poses while dancing—like flipping arms or legs!
  • Save and Name It: Click “File” &gt; “Save to your computer” or save it online if logged in. Name it “Dancing Cat Remix” or something cool. Congrats—you’ve made your first animation!

Final Code Sequence for Dancing Cat Animation

  • Events: “when green flag clicked”
  • Control: “repeat 10”
    • Motion: “move 50 steps”
    • Looks: “switch costume to cat-b”
    • Motion: “move -50 steps”
    • Looks: “switch costume to cat-a”
  • Looks: “say ‘I’m a dance machine!’ for 2 seconds”

Why This Matters

This simple project teaches you three huge coding ideas: sequencing (putting blocks in order to do things step-by-step), events (starting actions with triggers like the green flag), and repetition (using loops to repeat moves). Plus, you’ve just turned an idea into something real—how awesome is that? Animations are a great way to express yourself, whether you’re making a funny skit for friends or a short story for class.

Section 3: Understanding Loops, Conditionals, and Variables in Scratch

Coding Concepts Made Easy

Now that you’ve made something cool, let’s level up by learning three building blocks of coding: loops, conditionals, and variables. These sound fancy, but Scratch makes them super simple with visual blocks. They’re like the secret sauce that turns basic projects into interactive, dynamic creations. Let’s break them down with practical examples you can try right now!

Loops: Repeat the Fun

What Are Loops? Loops tell your program to repeat actions over and over without you dragging the same block a million times. It’s like setting a playlist on repeat—same song, multiple plays. Example in Scratch: In your Dancing Cat project, you used the “repeat 10” block to make the cat dance back and forth 10 times. Without a loop, you’d have to copy-paste “move 50 steps” and “move -50 steps” 10 times each—boring! Try It with Coding Steps: Open a new project. Pick a sprite (like a bird). Follow these steps to make it fly endlessly:

  • From “Events,” drag “when green flag clicked” to the Coding Area.
  • From “Motion,” drag “go to x:0 y:0” and snap it under the event block to start at the center.
  • From “Control,” drag a “forever” loop and snap it under “go to.”
  • Inside the “forever” loop, add “glide 1 secs to x:100 y:0” (flies right).
  • Add another “glide 1 secs to x:-100 y:0” (flies left) under the first glide. Final Code Sequence for Bird Loop:
  • Events: “when green flag clicked”
  • Motion: “go to x:0 y:0”
  • Control: “forever”
    • Motion: “glide 1 secs to x:100 y:0”
    • Motion: “glide 1 secs to x:-100 y:0” Hit the green flag—your bird flies side to side forever! Loops save time and make animations smooth. Teen Twist: Add a “play sound ‘chirp’ until done” block inside the loop. Now your bird chirps as it flies—perfect for annoying your sibling with endless bird noises!

Conditionals: Make Decisions

What Are Conditionals? Conditionals are like “if-then” rules—they let your program make choices based on what’s happening. Think of it as deciding “If it’s raining, then I’ll grab an umbrella.” Example in Scratch: Let’s make a sprite react to your clicks. Pick a sprite (like a dog). Follow these steps:

  • From “Events,” drag “when this sprite clicked” to the Coding Area.
  • From “Looks,” snap “say ‘Woof! You got me!’ for 2 seconds” under it. Final Code Sequence for Click Reaction:
  • Events: “when this sprite clicked”
  • Looks: “say ‘Woof! You got me!’ for 2 seconds” Test it—click the dog, and it talks! This is an “if” condition: if clicked, then speak. Try It with More Logic: Add more logic with “if-else” from “Control.” Follow these steps:
  • Drag “when green flag clicked” from “Events.”
  • Drag an “if-else” block from “Control” under it.
  • From “Sensing,” grab “mouse down?” and place it in the diamond slot of “if.”
  • Add “say ‘Clicking somewhere!’ for 1 sec” in the “if” part.
  • Add “say ‘Not clicking!’ for 1 sec” in the “else” part. Final Code Sequence for Mouse Check:
  • Events: “when green flag clicked”
  • Control: “if &lt;mouse down?&gt; then”
    • Looks: “say ‘Clicking somewhere!’ for 1 sec”
  • else
    • Looks: “say ‘Not clicking!’ for 1 sec” Test it—your sprite reacts based on your action! Teen Twist: Change the messages to something funny like “Yo, stop clicking!” and “Finally, some peace!” Conditionals make projects interactive—great for games or pranks.

Variables: Keep Track of Stuff

What Are Variables? Variables are like storage boxes for info (like scores, names, or numbers) that can change as your project runs. It’s like tracking your high score in a game—it updates when you play better. Example in Scratch: Let’s make a click counter. Pick a sprite. Follow these steps:

  • Go to “Variables” (orange blocks), click “Make a Variable,” name it “Clicks,” and click OK.
  • From “Events,” drag “when green flag clicked.”
  • Drag “set Clicks to 0” from “Variables” under it to start at zero.
  • From “Events,” drag “when this sprite clicked” to a new stack.
  • Under it, add “change Clicks by 1” from “Variables.”
  • Add “say [Clicks] for 1 second” (use the “join” block from “Operators” to show the number by joining “Clicks” with an empty space or text). Final Code Sequence for Click Counter:
  • Stack 1:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Variables: “set Clicks to 0”
  • Stack 2:
    • Events: “when this sprite clicked”
    • Variables: “change Clicks by 1”
    • Looks: “say [join Clicks ‘’] for 1 sec” Test it—click the sprite, and it counts how many times! Try It with Conditional: Add a conditional to react to many clicks:
  • Under “when this sprite clicked,” after “change Clicks by 1,” add an “if” block from “Control.”
  • From “Operators,” drag “&gt;” and set it to “Clicks &gt; 5” in the “if” condition.
  • Add “say ‘Wow, you’re click-crazy!’ for 1 sec” inside the “if.” Updated Code Sequence for Click Counter with Conditional:
  • Stack 1:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Variables: “set Clicks to 0”
  • Stack 2:
    • Events: “when this sprite clicked”
    • Variables: “change Clicks by 1”
    • Looks: “say [join Clicks ‘’] for 1 sec”
    • Control: “if &lt;Clicks &gt; 5&gt; then”
      • Looks: “say ‘Wow, you’re click-crazy!’ for 1 sec” Variables track data, making projects dynamic. Teen Twist: Turn it into a challenge—tell friends to get to 10 clicks fastest. Variables are key for games (like scores) or apps (like tracking steps).

Why These Matter to You

Loops, conditionals, and variables are the heart of coding—they let you create projects that repeat actions, react to users, and store info. Whether you’re making a game, quiz, or story, these concepts give you power to build stuff that feels alive. Plus, they’re used in every coding language, so learning them now in Scratch sets you up for Python or JavaScript later!

  • Create a variable “Scares” in “Variables.”
  • For floating: Use “when green flag clicked,” “go to x:0 y:0,” then a “forever” loop with “change y by 10” and “change y by -10” (add “wait 0.5 secs” between for smooth motion).

  • Stack 1:

    • Motion: “go to x:0 y:0”
    • Control: “forever”
      • Motion: “change y by 10”
      • Control: “wait 0.5 secs”
      • Motion: “change y by -10”
      • Control: “wait 0.5 secs”
  • Stack 2:
    • Events: “when this sprite clicked”

Section 4: Building a Basic Game in Scratch (e.g., Maze or Pong)

Time to Build a Game!

You’ve got the basics down, so let’s create something you can play—a simple Pong game! Pong is a classic where a ball bounces between two paddles, and you score by getting past your opponent. It’s perfect for learning game design in Scratch, and you can play it with a friend. This project pulls together everything you’ve learned (motion, conditionals, variables) and adds game logic. Let’s make it step by step!

Step-by-Step: Creating Pong with Coding Steps

  • Set Up the Scene: Start a new project on Scratch. Choose a plain backdrop (like “Blue Sky”). Add two paddle sprites—use rectangles from the Paint Editor (draw tall, thin shapes) or pick something like “Paddle” from the library. Name them “Paddle1” (left) and “Paddle2” (right). Add a ball sprite (like “Ball” from the library or draw a circle).
  • Position the Paddles: For Paddle1, drag it to the left side of the stage. In “Motion,” use the following code to lock its starting spot:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Motion: “go to x:-200 y:0” Do the same for Paddle2:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Motion: “go to x:200 y:0” This sets up the “court.”
  • Control Paddles with Keys: For Paddle1, add movement controls:
    • Stack 1:
      • Events: “when up arrow key pressed”
      • Motion: “change y by 10”
    • Stack 2:
      • Events: “when down arrow key pressed”
      • Motion: “change y by -10” For Paddle2, use different keys:
    • Stack 1:
      • Events: “when w key pressed”
      • Motion: “change y by 10”
    • Stack 2:
      • Events: “when s key pressed”
      • Motion: “change y by -10” Test it—press keys to move paddles!
  • Make the Ball Move: For the Ball sprite, add code to move continuously:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Motion: “go to x:0 y:0”
    • Control: “forever”
      • Motion: “move 5 steps”
      • Motion: “if on edge, bounce” This makes the ball zip around and bounce off walls. Adjust speed by changing “5” if it’s too fast/slow.
  • Bounce Off Paddles: Still on the Ball, inside the “forever” loop, add conditions for bouncing:
    • Inside “forever” loop, add:
      • Control: “if &lt;touching Paddle1?&gt; then”
        • Motion: “turn 180 degrees”
      • Control: “if &lt;touching Paddle2?&gt; then”
        • Motion: “turn 180 degrees” Test it—ball should bounce off paddles!
  • Score Points: Make two variables in “Variables”—“Score1” (for Paddle1) and “Score2” (for Paddle2). Add scoring logic:
    • For Ball sprite, under “when green flag clicked,” before the “forever” loop:
      • Variables: “set Score1 to 0”
      • Variables: “set Score2 to 0”
    • Inside the “forever” loop, after paddle checks, add:
      • Control: “if &lt;x position &gt; 240&gt; then”
        • Variables: “change Score1 by 1”
        • Looks: “say ‘Point for Player 1!’ for 1 sec”
        • Motion: “go to x:0 y:0”
      • Control: “if &lt;x position &lt; -240&gt; then”
        • Variables: “change Score2 by 1”
        • Looks: “say ‘Point for Player 2!’ for 1 sec”
        • Motion: “go to x:0 y:0” Test it—scores update!
  • Polish It: Add a “Broadcast” when a point is scored to play a sound:
    • Inside each “if” for scoring, add:
      • Events: “broadcast ‘point’”
    • Add a new sprite (or use Ball) with:
      • Events: “when I receive ‘point’”
      • Sound: “play sound ‘pop’ until done” Display scores on stage by checking “Show” for the variables in the Variables menu. Name your project “Epic Pong Battle” and save it.

Section 5: Sharing and Collaborating on Scratch Projects

Show Off Your Creations!

You’ve built animations and a game—now let’s share them with the world! Scratch isn’t just a tool; it’s a community where millions of creators (many your age) share projects, give feedback, and remix each other’s work. Sharing is a great way to get inspired, learn new tricks, and even make online friends who love coding as much as you do. Plus, showing your projects can boost your confidence and maybe even impress a teacher or future college!

How to Share on Scratch

  • Save Your Project: Make sure your project (like your Dancing Cat or Pong) is saved. If you’re logged in, click “Save Now” to store it online in your Scratch account.
  • Publish It: At the top of the project editor, click the “Share” button. This makes your project public on the Scratch website so others can see and play it. Don’t worry—you can unshare later if you change your mind.
  • Add Details: Before sharing, give your project a cool name (like “Ultimate Dance Party” or “Pong Showdown”). Add a short description in the “Instructions” box (e.g., “Click to make the cat dance!”) and “Notes and Credits” (like “Thanks to my BFF for the idea!”). Tag it with keywords like “animation” or “game” so others can find it.
  • Share the Link: Once shared, you’ll get a unique link (like scratch.mit.edu/projects/123456). Copy it and send it to friends via text, Discord, or social media. You can also embed it in a blog or school site if you have one!
  • Explore the Community: Click “Explore” on Scratch’s homepage to see trending projects. Comment on ones you like (e.g., “Love the graphics!”) and click “Favorite” or “Love” to save them. You’ll get notifications if people comment on yours too!

Remixing: Build on Others’ Ideas

One of Scratch’s coolest features is “remixing”—taking someone else’s project and adding your own spin. Click “See Inside” on a shared project, then “Remix” to make a copy you can edit. Maybe add a new character, change the music, or tweak the rules of a game. Always give credit in the “Notes and Credits” section (e.g., “Remixed from @username’s awesome game!”). It’s like collabing on a TikTok dance—everyone adds their flavor! Here’s how to remix with a small coding tweak:

  • Open a shared project you like (e.g., a simple animation).
  • Click “Remix” to create your version.
  • Add a new feature, like a sound effect:
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Sound: “play sound ‘cheer’ until done”
  • Save and share your remixed version with credit to the original creator.

Collaborating with Friends

Got a coding buddy? Work together! Scratch lets you share projects privately before publishing by sending the link while it’s unshared (or use “Backpack” to copy sprites/blocks between projects). Plan a project with a friend—like a story where you each animate a character. Meet up online or in person to brainstorm. Two heads are better than one, and you’ll learn from each other’s ideas. Some teens even form “Scratch Studios” (like group pages) to team up on big projects—search “Studios” on Scratch to join one! Here’s a quick way to collab with coding steps:

  • Create a joint project, like a dialogue between two sprites.
  • Assign one sprite to each person. For example, Sprite1 (handled by you):
    • Events: “when green flag clicked”
    • Looks: “say ‘Hey, what’s up?’ for 2 secs”
    • Control: “wait 2 secs”
    • Events: “broadcast ‘reply’”
  • Your friend codes Sprite2:
    • Events: “when I receive ‘reply’”
    • Looks: “say ‘Not much, you?’ for 2 secs”
  • Save and test together, tweaking the timing or messages as needed.

Safety First

Scratch is teen-friendly, but keep it chill online. Don’t share personal info (like your real name or address) in projects or comments. If someone’s rude, click “Report” on their comment or profile. Stick to fun, positive vibes—think of it like posting on Insta or Snap, but for coding. Your Scratch account is your creative space, so protect it!
