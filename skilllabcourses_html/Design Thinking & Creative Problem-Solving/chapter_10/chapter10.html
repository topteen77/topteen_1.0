Capstone Project – Solving a High School Challenge with Tech

 

Section 1: Picking a Real High School Issue to Solve (e.g., Organizing Study Time, Reducing Stress)

What’s Bugging You and Your Friends? The first step in your Capstone Project is picking a real problem that high schoolers face every day. Maybe you’re drowning in homework and can’t keep track of deadlines, or stress from exams is messing with your sleep. This section is about identifying a challenge that matters to you and your peers—something you’re passionate about fixing. As high schoolers, you know better than anyone what struggles hit hardest, so let’s use that insider knowledge to pick a project worth solving.

Why Pick a High School Issue? Choosing a problem close to your life makes this project personal and motivating. If it’s something you deal with—like juggling sports, school, and social stuff—you’ll care more about finding a solution. Plus, solving a high school challenge means you can test your idea with classmates who get it, making your Design Thinking process super real and relatable. This isn’t about fixing world hunger (yet!); it’s about making high school life better, one step at a time.

 

How to Pick Your Issue with Design Thinking: Follow these steps to zero in on the perfect problem:

  • Brainstorm Challenges: List 3-5 issues you or your friends face in high school. Think about academics (like study time), mental health (like stress or anxiety), social stuff (like bullying), or even logistics (like getting to school on time).
  • Empathize with Peers: Talk to a few classmates or friends. Ask, “What’s one thing about high school that drives you crazy?” or “What do you wish was easier?” See if their struggles match yours.
  • Narrow It Down: Pick one issue that feels most important or solvable. Ask, “Can I imagine a tech solution for this?” and “Do I care enough to work on it?”
  • Define the Problem: Write a clear statement, like “High schoolers need a way to organize study time so they don’t miss deadlines.” This keeps your project focused.

    •  

Examples in Action:

  • Organizing Study Time: You notice you and your friends keep forgetting assignments or cramming last minute. Your problem statement: “High schoolers need a better way to plan study time to stay on top of schoolwork.”
  • Reducing Stress: Exams make everyone anxious, and nobody knows how to chill. Your problem statement: “High schoolers need tools to manage stress during test season to feel calmer.”

Why This Matters for You: Picking a real issue builds your empathy and problem-solving skills—stuff that looks great on college apps and prepares you for future challenges. Plus, solving something you care about keeps you hyped to finish this project strong.

Section 2: Using Digital or AI Tools to Create a Solution (e.g., a Study Schedule App, AI Summarizer)

How Can Tech Help Fix This? Now that you’ve picked your high school issue, it’s time to get creative with technology! This section is about designing a solution using digital tools or AI—think apps, websites, or even simple tech hacks that make life easier. As high schoolers, you’re already glued to your phones and laptops, so let’s use that to build something useful with Design Thinking. You don’t need to be a coding genius; there are tons of user-friendly tools out there to help you create awesome stuff.

Why Use Tech for Solutions? Technology can make solutions faster, more accessible, and way cooler. A study app can send reminders to your phone so you never forget a test. An AI tool can summarize long readings in seconds, saving you time. Tech also lets you reach more people—your solution could help not just you, but tons of classmates. Plus, learning to use digital tools now preps you for college and careers where tech skills are a must.

 

How to Create a Tech Solution with Design Thinking: Here’s how to brainstorm and build your idea:

  • Ideate Tech Options: Think about what kind of tech could solve your problem. Apps for scheduling? AI for summarizing notes? A group chat bot for stress-relief tips? List 2-3 possibilities.
  • Research Tools: Look up free or easy-to-use platforms. For apps, try Canva for designs or Google Forms for surveys. For AI, check out tools like ChatGPT for brainstorming or summarizing (with teacher approval).
  • Prototype Your Solution: Create a rough version. If it’s a study schedule app, mock up a calendar layout in Google Sheets with reminders. If it’s an AI summarizer, test summarizing a chapter and share the output. Keep it simple—you’re just testing the idea!
  • Test with Peers: Show your prototype to a friend or classmate. Ask, “Does this help with the issue? What could be better?” Use their feedback to tweak it.

    •  

Examples in Action:

  • Study Schedule App: For the problem of organizing study time, you design a simple app layout using a free tool like Canva. It includes a weekly planner with alerts for homework deadlines and study blocks. You test it by sharing a PDF version with friends.
  • AI Summarizer: To reduce stress from heavy reading, you use an AI tool to summarize textbook chapters into short bullet points. You save these summaries in a shared doc for your study group and ask if it saves them time.

Why This Matters for You: Building a tech solution teaches you to think like an innovator and boosts your digital skills—huge for future jobs in a tech-driven world. It also shows you how to turn ideas into real tools that help people, which feels pretty epic.

Section 3: Thinking About the Right and Wrong Ways to Use Your Chosen Tool

Is Your Tech Solution Helping or Hurting? Technology is powerful, but it’s not always perfect. This section is about thinking critically about how you use digital or AI tools in your Capstone Project. As high schoolers, you’ve seen tech do amazing things (like connecting with friends) and not-so-great things (like spreading rumors online). Let’s make sure your solution helps without causing new problems, using Design Thinking to evaluate the “right” and “wrong” ways to use your tool.

Why Think About Right and Wrong Use? Tech can have unintended consequences if not used thoughtfully. An app might help with study time but distract you with notifications. AI might summarize readings but could be misused to skip learning altogether. Thinking about ethics and responsibility ensures your solution truly benefits people and doesn’t create harm. Plus, being a responsible tech user shows maturity—a skill colleges and employers value.

 

How to Evaluate Your Tech Tool with Design Thinking: Use these steps to assess your solution:

  • List Benefits (Right Ways): Write down how your tool helps solve the problem. Does it save time? Reduce stress? Make things easier for classmates? Be specific.
  • Spot Risks (Wrong Ways): Think about how it could be misused or cause issues. Could it distract users? Lead to cheating? Share private info? Brainstorm 2-3 potential downsides.
  • Empathize with Users: Ask, “How might my classmates feel if this tool goes wrong?” Imagine their perspective if data gets leaked or if the tool wastes their time.
  • Plan Fixes: For each risk, suggest a way to minimize it. For example, add a “focus mode” to disable notifications on an app, or include a note with AI summaries saying, “Use this as a study aid, not a shortcut.”

    •  

Examples in Action:

  • Study Schedule App:
    • Right Way: Helps students plan study sessions and sends helpful reminders for deadlines.
    • Wrong Way: Overloads users with notifications, stressing them out more, or tracks data without permission.
    • Fix: Limit notifications to key times and add a privacy notice saying no data is stored.
  • AI Summarizer:
    • Right Way: Cuts down reading time by summarizing tough chapters for review.
    • Wrong Way: Students might rely on it instead of reading, missing key learning, or pass off summaries as their own work.
    • Fix: Include a disclaimer to read originals first and use summaries only for revision.

Why This Matters for You: Thinking about ethical tech use builds critical thinking and responsibility—skills that make you stand out as a leader. It also ensures your project has a positive impact, not a messy one, which feels way better.

 

Activity for Practice: For your tech solution, list 2 “right ways” it helps and 2 “wrong ways” it could be misused. Suggest a fix for each wrong way.

Section 4: Sharing Your Idea with Classmates in a Short Presentation

Show Off Your Tech Solution! You’ve picked a problem, built a tech solution, and thought about how to use it responsibly—now it’s time to share your idea with classmates! This section is about creating a short presentation to explain your Capstone Project, get feedback, and inspire others. As high schoolers, you’ve probably done class presentations before, but this one’s special—it’s your chance to show how you’re making high school life better with tech.

Why Share Your Idea? Presenting your project helps you practice communication skills, which are huge for college interviews and jobs. It also lets you get feedback from classmates to improve your solution (that’s the “Test” stage of Design Thinking!). Plus, sharing ideas can motivate others to tackle challenges with tech, creating a ripple effect of innovation in your class.

 

How to Build and Deliver Your Presentation: Follow these steps to make a killer short presentation (3-5 minutes):

  • Structure Your Talk: Keep it simple with 3 parts:
    • Intro (1 min): Say your name, the high school issue you picked, and why it matters. Example: “I’m tackling study time chaos because we all struggle with deadlines.”
    • Solution (2-3 min): Explain your tech tool, how it works, and one “right” and “wrong” way to use it. Show your prototype if possible (like a screenshot or demo).
    • Closing (1 min): Share what you learned and ask for feedback. Example: “What do you think could make this app even better?”
  • Use Visuals: Create 3-4 slides on PowerPoint, Google Slides, or Canva. Include a title slide, a problem slide, a solution slide, and a feedback slide. Add pics or mockups of your tool!
  • Practice: Rehearse 2-3 times to stay within 5 minutes. Speak clearly, smile, and don’t just read off slides—talk like you’re chatting with friends.
  • Engage Your Audience: End by asking a question like, “Would you use this tool? Why or why not?” Listen to their ideas to improve your project.

Example in Action:

  • Project: Study Schedule App.
  • Presentation: You start by saying, “I’m tired of forgetting homework, so I made a study app!” Show a mockup of the app with a weekly planner. Explain it sends reminders (right way) but could distract with too many alerts (wrong way). End by asking, “What features would you add to this app?” Classmates suggest a “mute” option for nights, which you note for improvement.

    •  

Why This Matters for You: Presenting builds confidence and public speaking skills—stuff you’ll use forever. Plus, feedback from classmates can take your project from good to great, showing you the power of collaboration.

 

Activity for Practice: Outline your short presentation for your tech solution. Write 1-2 sentences for each part (Intro, Solution, Closing) and note one visual you’d include.

Section 5: Setting Goals to Keep Improving Your Tech Skills Before Graduation

What’s Next for Your Tech Journey? Your Capstone Project is a huge step, but it’s just the start of using tech to solve problems. This section is about setting goals to grow your digital and AI skills before you graduate high school. As teens, you’ve got time to learn, experiment, and become tech wizards—skills that’ll give you a head start in college or careers. Let’s think about how to keep leveling up after this project.

Why Set Tech Goals? Technology changes fast, and staying ahead means you’re ready for anything—whether it’s coding a game, designing apps, or using AI for schoolwork. Setting goals now keeps you motivated and builds a habit of learning, which is key in a world where tech is everywhere. Plus, strong tech skills make you stand out on resumes and open doors to cool opportunities.

 

How to Set Tech Skill Goals: Use these steps to plan your growth:

  • Reflect on Your Project: Think about what tech skills you used or struggled with in your Capstone. Did designing an app mockup spark interest in coding? Was using AI tricky but exciting? Write down one skill you liked or want to improve.
  • Set SMART Goals: Make goals Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Example: “I’ll learn basic coding on Codecademy for 30 minutes a week until June to build a simple app.”
  • Find Resources: Look for free or school-provided tools. Try Khan Academy for coding, YouTube for app design tutorials, or ask a teacher about tech clubs. List 1-2 ways to learn.
  • Track Progress: Decide how you’ll check in. Maybe log hours spent learning or share mini-projects with friends. Set a date before graduation to review your growth.

    •  

Examples in Action:

  • Goal After Study App Project: “I’ll complete a free app design course on Coursera by May to learn how to make my study app real. I’ll practice 1 hour weekly and show my teacher a draft by April.”
  • Goal After AI Summarizer Project: “I’ll explore ethical AI use by reading one article a month and joining a school tech club by next semester to discuss AI’s impact with peers.”

Why This Matters for You: Setting tech goals builds a growth mindset—showing you can adapt and learn, which is huge for future success. It also preps you for a world where tech skills are as important as reading or math, giving you confidence to tackle anything after high school.
