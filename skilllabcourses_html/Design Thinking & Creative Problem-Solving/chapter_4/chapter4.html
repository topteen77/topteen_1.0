Chapter 4: Ideation – Brainstorming Creative Solutions

 

Section 1- What is Ideation? Unleashing Creativity Without Limits

Let’s kick off by understanding what Ideation is and why it’s such a crucial part of Design Thinking. Ideation is the stage where you brainstorm and generate a wide range of potential solutions to the problem you defined in Chapter 3. It’s about coming up with as many ideas as you can—big, small, practical, or completely crazy—without worrying about whether they’re “good” or “possible” just yet. The goal is to explore every corner of your imagination and open up possibilities you might not have considered before. As a high school student, this is your chance to think like an inventor, artist, or dreamer, and apply that creativity to real-world challenges.

 

Why Ideation Matters: Ideation is the bridge between identifying a problem and creating a solution. Without this stage, you might jump to the first obvious fix and miss out on innovative, game-changing ideas. For example, if your problem statement is “Freshmen need a quicker way to pay for lunch because long lines cut into their break time,” ideation lets you brainstorm everything from a new payment app to a pre-order system to a completely reimagined cafeteria setup. By thinking broadly at this stage, you increase your chances of finding a solution that’s not just okay, but truly awesome. Plus, ideation builds skills like creative thinking and problem-solving that you can use in school projects, future careers, or even personal hobbies.

 

Key Principles of Ideation: Here are a few core ideas to keep in mind as you dive into this stage:

  • No Judgment: During ideation, there’s no such thing as a “bad” idea. Don’t criticize your own thoughts or anyone else’s—every idea is a stepping stone to something better.
  • Quantity Over Quality (For Now): Focus on generating lots of ideas rather than perfect ones. The more ideas you have, the more likely you are to stumble on something unique.
  • Build on Others’ Ideas: If you’re working in a team, listen to your peers and add to their suggestions with “Yes, and…” instead of “No, but…” This keeps the energy positive and collaborative.
  • Think Outside the Box: Don’t limit yourself to what’s realistic or easy. Imagine there are no rules, no budget constraints, and no limits to technology or resources. What would you create?
  • User-Centered Focus: Keep your problem statement and the user’s needs in mind. Every idea should somehow connect back to solving the challenge for the specific group you identified.

    •  

How Ideation Works in Practice: Ideation usually starts with your problem statement or “How Might We?” (HMW) question from Chapter 3 as your guide. For instance, if your HMW question is “How might we speed up cafeteria lines so freshmen have more time to eat during lunch?” you’d brainstorm any and all ways to address that need. Ideas might range from practical (a faster payment system) to wild (a robot server handing out food). You can ideate alone or in a group, using structured techniques (which we’ll cover next) or just free-flowing thoughts. The key is to let creativity flow without overthinking.

Example in Action:

  • Problem Statement: “High school athletes need more accessible storage for sports gear because cramped lockers cause frustration before practice.”
  • Ideation Goal: Come up with 20+ ways to solve this, like portable gear carts, expandable lockers, a gear-sharing app, or even a futuristic drone that delivers equipment to the field. No idea is off-limits!

    •  

Activity for Practice: Grab a piece of paper and set a timer for 5 minutes. Write down your problem statement or HMW question from Chapter 3 (or create a simple one, like “Students need a way to stay organized”). Brainstorm as many solutions as you can, no matter how silly or impossible they seem. Aim for at least 10 ideas. When the timer’s up, look at your list—did any surprise you? Share one wild idea with a friend and see what they think.

Ideation is all about unleashing creativity without limits. It’s a safe space to dream big and explore what’s possible, setting the stage for innovative solutions. As a high schooler, this process can help you see that your imagination is a powerful tool for solving problems in ways no one else has thought of. Let’s keep that creative energy going as we explore specific brainstorming techniques next!

 

Section 2- Brainstorming Techniques: Mind Mapping, SCAMPER, and Crazy 8s

Now that you understand what ideation is, let’s dive into some specific brainstorming techniques to help you generate tons of ideas. These methods are like tools in your creativity toolkit—they give structure to your thinking and help spark inspiration when you’re feeling stuck. We’ll explore three popular techniques that are perfect for high school students: Mind Mapping, SCAMPER, and Crazy 8s. Each one offers a different way to approach your problem statement or HMW question, ensuring you come up with diverse and innovative solutions.

  • 1. Mind Mapping – Visualizing Connections: Mind Mapping is a visual brainstorming tool that helps you organize thoughts and explore related ideas around a central theme. It’s great for seeing how different concepts connect and for sparking new directions.
  • How It Works:
    • Write your problem statement or HMW question in the center of a blank page (e.g., “How might we speed up cafeteria lines?”). Draw a circle around it.
    • Branch out with main categories or themes related to the problem (like “Technology,” “Layout,” “Payment,” “Timing”). Draw lines connecting these to the center.
    • For each category, add smaller branches with specific ideas or subtopics (under “Technology,” you might write “Mobile app,” “QR codes,” “Digital tickets”).
    • Keep branching with more details or related thoughts as they come to you. Use colors, doodles, or symbols to make it fun and engaging.
  • Why It’s Useful: Mind Mapping helps you see the big picture and uncover connections you might miss in a linear list. It’s perfect for visual learners and group brainstorming.
  • Example: For cafeteria lines, a branch under “Layout” might lead to “Separate lines for hot/cold food,” which could spark “Pre-packaged grab-and-go meals.”
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Use Mind Mapping for school projects or studying too—it’s a great way to brainstorm essay topics or connect historical events.
  • 2. SCAMPER – Remixing Existing Ideas: SCAMPER is a creative thinking technique that stands for Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, and Reverse. It prompts you to tweak existing ideas or systems to create new solutions.
  • How It Works: Take your problem or an existing solution and ask questions based on each SCAMPER letter:
    • Substitute: What can I replace to make this better? (e.g., “Replace cash payments with a card swipe system for cafeteria lines.”)
    • Combine: What can I merge to improve it? (“Combine lunch orders with a school app for pre-ordering.”)
    • Adapt: How can I adjust this for my context? (“Adapt a fast-food drive-thru model for school lunch pickup.”)
    • Modify: How can I change the size, shape, or process? (“Modify the cafeteria layout for multiple entry points.”)
    • Put to Another Use: Can this be used differently? (“Use library study rooms as overflow seating during lunch.”)
    • Eliminate: What can I remove to simplify? (“Eliminate long wait times by cutting unnecessary menu options.”)
    • Reverse: What if I flip the process? (“Reverse the flow—students pick food first, then pay at the end.”)
  • Why It’s Useful: SCAMPER forces you to look at a problem from different angles and rethink what’s already there. It’s awesome when you’re stuck or want to innovate on something familiar.
  • Example: For gear storage, “Eliminate” might lead to “Remove lockers entirely and use a shared gear room with sign-outs.”
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Try SCAMPER on everyday objects (like a backpack) to practice creative thinking—it’s a fun way to build this skill.
  • 3. Crazy 8s – Rapid Idea Generation: Crazy 8s is a fast-paced sketching exercise to generate lots of ideas in a short time. It’s perfect for visual thinkers and for pushing past mental blocks.
  • How It Works:
    • Take a piece of paper and fold it into 8 sections (or draw 8 boxes).
    • Set a timer for 8 minutes.
    • Sketch one idea per box to solve your problem—spend just 1 minute per idea. Don’t overthink; draw quick concepts (stick figures and shapes are fine).
    • Focus on variety—make each idea different from the last.
  • Why It’s Useful: Crazy 8s gets you to churn out ideas quickly without judging them. The time pressure helps silence your inner critic and boosts creativity.
  • Example: For cafeteria lines, sketches might include a conveyor belt for food, a ticket system, a student-led “express line,” or a food truck outside.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: You don’t need to be an artist—simple doodles work. Use this when you’re short on time but need a burst of ideas.

Activity for Practice: Pick one of these techniques (Mind Mapping, SCAMPER, or Crazy 8s) and apply it to your problem statement or HMW question from Chapter 3 (or a new one, like “How might we make homework less stressful?”). Spend 10-15 minutes on it. If you’re in a group, try one together. Afterward, write down 2-3 favorite ideas you came up with and why they stand out to you. Share with a classmate for feedback.

These brainstorming techniques—Mind Mapping, SCAMPER, and Crazy 8s—are powerful ways to fuel your ideation process. They help you think visually, remix ideas, and generate solutions fast, ensuring you don’t get stuck on just one way of solving a problem. Experiment with all three to find which works best for you, and remember: the goal is to let creativity flow without limits!

 

 

Section 3- Encouraging Wild Ideas: Quantity Over Quality

One of the most important mindsets in the ideation stage is to focus on quantity over quality and to encourage wild ideas. This might sound counterintuitive—aren’t we supposed to aim for the best solutions? Not yet! During ideation, the goal isn’t to perfect your ideas but to generate as many as possible, including ones that seem far-fetched, impractical, or downright crazy. As a high school student, this approach frees you from the pressure of “getting it right” and lets you tap into your boldest, most creative thoughts to solve problems in unexpected ways.

Why Focus on Quantity Over Quality?

  • More Ideas, More Possibilities: The more ideas you brainstorm, the higher the chance you’ll stumble on something truly innovative. Even “bad” ideas can spark a better one later.
  • Breaking Mental Barriers: Worrying about quality early on can stop you from thinking freely. Focusing on quantity pushes you past fear of failure or judgment.
  • Wild Ideas Lead to Innovation: Crazy, unrealistic ideas often inspire practical solutions. For example, imagining “a flying cafeteria” might lead to a more doable idea like “mobile food carts.”
  • Building Confidence: Generating lots of ideas shows you that creativity isn’t about being perfect—it’s about exploring. This builds your confidence as a problem-solver.

What Are Wild Ideas and Why Do They Matter? Wild ideas are solutions that ignore real-world constraints like budget, technology, or rules. They’re the “what if” thoughts that seem impossible—like “What if we had a robot teacher for every student?” or “What if the school bus was a teleportation device?” They matter because:

  • They stretch your imagination and push you to think beyond the obvious.
  • They can be toned down into feasible solutions (a robot teacher might become a personalized learning app).
  • They make brainstorming fun and energizing, especially in teams, keeping everyone engaged.

    •  

How to Encourage Wild Ideas: Here are some tips to embrace quantity and wildness in your ideation sessions:

  • Set a High Goal: Challenge yourself or your team to come up with 20, 30, or even 50 ideas in a short time (like 10 minutes). Don’t stop until you hit the number.
  • Ban Criticism: Make a rule—no one can say “That won’t work” or “That’s stupid” during brainstorming. Every idea is welcome.
  • Use Prompts for Wildness: Ask questions like “What if money wasn’t an issue?” “What if we lived in a sci-fi world?” or “What’s the weirdest way to solve this?”
  • Build on Ideas: If someone suggests a wild idea, add to it with “Yes, and…” For example, if a teammate says, “What if we had floating lockers?” you might add, “Yes, and they could follow students to class!”
  • Laugh and Have Fun: Wild ideas often lead to laughter, and that’s a good thing! A positive vibe keeps creativity flowing.

    •  

Example in Action:

  • Problem Statement: “High school athletes need more accessible storage for sports gear because cramped lockers cause frustration.”
  • Wild Ideas: “What if gear was stored in a giant inflatable bubble that pops up on the field?” “What if drones delivered gear to each player?” “What if uniforms were made of shape-shifting fabric that appears when needed?”
  • Toning Down: The drone idea might inspire a more realistic “gear delivery cart system” managed by student volunteers.

    •  

Activity for Practice: Take your problem statement or HMW question and set a timer for 10 minutes. Brainstorm at least 20 ideas, aiming for at least 5 to be completely wild (ignore all real-world limits). Write them down without stopping to judge. Afterward, circle one wild idea and think of a way to make it more practical. Share with a friend or group and see if they can build on it with “Yes, and…”

Encouraging wild ideas and focusing on quantity over quality is the heart of ideation. It’s about giving yourself permission to dream without limits and trusting that even the craziest thoughts can lead to brilliant solutions. As a high schooler, this mindset can help you tackle challenges with confidence and originality—skills that will shine in any project or future career. Let’s keep that creative energy alive as we explore teamwork next!

 

 

Section 4- Collaborating in Teams for Diverse Perspectives

Ideation isn’t just a solo activity—it’s often at its best when done in teams. Collaborating with others brings diverse perspectives to the table, helping you generate more ideas and see your problem from angles you might not have considered on your own. As a high school student, working in teams during ideation teaches you valuable skills like communication, listening, and combining strengths—skills that are useful in group projects, extracurriculars, and beyond. In this section, we’ll explore why team collaboration matters in Design Thinking and how to make the most of it during brainstorming.

Why Collaborate in Teams During Ideation?

  • Diverse Perspectives Spark Better Ideas: Everyone has unique experiences, backgrounds, and ways of thinking. A teammate might see a solution you’d never imagine because of their different viewpoint. For example, if you’re brainstorming ways to improve school events, someone who’s shy might suggest ideas for introverts that an outgoing person wouldn’t think of.
  • Building on Ideas: Teams can use the “Yes, and…” approach to expand on each other’s thoughts, turning a simple idea into something extraordinary. Collaboration creates a snowball effect of creativity.
  • Shared Energy and Motivation: Brainstorming with others is often more fun and energizing than doing it alone. A lively group can keep ideas flowing even when you’re feeling stuck.
  • Real-World Skill: Working in teams mirrors how many innovations happen in the real world—through collaboration. Learning to ideate together prepares you for future group work in college or jobs.

    •  

How to Collaborate Effectively in Teams: Here are some tips to ensure your team ideation sessions are productive and inclusive:

  • Start with the Problem Statement: Make sure everyone understands the problem statement or HMW question from Chapter 3. Write it on a whiteboard or paper where everyone can see it to stay focused.
  • Set Ground Rules: Agree on rules like “No judgment,” “Quantity over quality,” and “Everyone contributes.” This creates a safe space for sharing ideas, even wild ones.
  • Encourage Equal Participation: Make sure quieter team members have a chance to speak. Go around in a circle for one round of ideas, or use sticky notes so everyone can write thoughts anonymously if they’re shy.
  • Use Brainstorming Techniques Together: Try methods like Mind Mapping or Crazy 8s as a group. For Mind Mapping, one person can draw while others shout out branches. For Crazy 8s, everyone sketches individually, then shares.
  • Build on Ideas with “Yes, and…”: When someone shares an idea, others should add to it rather than criticize. For example, if a teammate says, “What if we had a school app for lunch orders?” you might say, “Yes, and it could let you customize meals in advance!”
  • Celebrate Diversity: Value different perspectives—someone’s cultural background, hobbies, or personal challenges might lead to a unique solution. Ask questions like “What’s something you’ve experienced that could help solve this?”
  • Keep It Fun: Use humour, draw silly ideas, or play music to keep the mood light. A positive vibe helps creativity flow.

    •  

     

Example in Action:

  • Problem Statement: “Freshmen need a quicker way to pay for lunch because long lines cut into their break time.”
  • Team Ideation: A group of four students brainstorms. One, who’s tech-savvy, suggests a payment app. Another, who works part-time at a fast-food place, builds on it with “Yes, and we could have a QR code system like at my job.” A third, who’s on the student council, adds, “Yes, and we could get volunteers to scan codes during peak hours.” A fourth, who’s shy but loves art, sketches a fun app interface. Together, their diverse perspectives create a richer idea than any one person could alone.

    •  

Activity for Practice: Form a small team (3-5 classmates or friends). Pick a problem statement or HMW question (from Chapter 3 or a new one, like “How might we make school hallways less crowded?”). Set a timer for 15 minutes and brainstorm as a group using one technique from the previous section (like Mind Mapping). Follow the “Yes, and…” rule and aim for at least 20 ideas. Afterward, write down one idea that came from someone else’s perspective that you wouldn’t have thought of alone, and reflect on how teamwork helped.

Collaborating in teams for diverse perspectives makes ideation more dynamic and effective. It’s about combining everyone’s unique strengths to create solutions that are richer and more inclusive. As a high schooler, mastering teamwork in this stage will not only help with Design Thinking projects but also prepare you for group challenges in any area of life. Now, let’s move on to narrowing down all those amazing ideas!

 

Section 5- Selecting the Best Ideas: Voting and Prioritization Methods

After generating a huge pool of ideas through brainstorming and collaboration, the next step in the ideation stage is selecting the best ideas to move forward with. You can’t prototype or test every single idea (at least not yet!), so you need a way to narrow down your list to the most promising ones. This doesn’t mean throwing out the wild ideas—sometimes they’re the most innovative—but it does mean focusing on solutions that best address your problem statement while being feasible and impactful. As a high school student, learning to prioritize ideas teaches you decision-making skills that are super useful for projects, time management, and even choosing future goals. Let’s explore some simple voting and prioritization methods to help you pick the winners.

 

Why Selecting Ideas Matters:

  • Focus Your Energy: You’ve got limited time and resources, so narrowing down helps you work on ideas with the highest potential rather than spreading yourself too thin.
  • Stay User-Centered: Prioritizing ensures you choose ideas that truly meet the needs of the user group from your problem statement, not just the ones you personally like.
  • Balance Creativity and Feasibility: This step lets you keep innovative ideas while considering what’s realistic to prototype and test in the next Design Thinking stages.
  • Build Team Consensus: If you’re working in a group, selecting ideas together ensures everyone agrees on the direction and feels invested in the next steps.

How to Select the Best Ideas: Here are three easy and effective methods to prioritize your brainstormed ideas. You can use one or combine them depending on your needs or team size.

  • 1. Dot Voting – Quick and Democratic: Dot Voting is a simple way to see which ideas have the most support, especially in teams.
  • How It Works:
    • Write or display all your ideas on a board, wall, or large paper (use sticky notes if you’ve got lots).
    • Give each person a set number of “dots” (stickers, marker dots, or checkmarks)—usually 3-5 per person.
    • Everyone places their dots on the ideas they think are the strongest. You can put all dots on one idea or spread them out.
    • Count the dots—the ideas with the most votes move forward (pick the top 2-3 depending on your project).
  • Why It’s Useful: It’s fast, visual, and lets everyone have a say without long debates. It’s great for groups of any size.
  • Example: For “speeding up cafeteria lines,” ideas like “QR code payment” (8 dots), “pre-order app” (6 dots), and “extra cashiers” (5 dots) might top the list.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Use colourful stickers for fun, and do a quick “Why did you vote for this?” round to understand everyone’s reasoning.
  • 2. Impact vs. Feasibility Matrix – Balancing Creativity and Practicality: This method helps you evaluate ideas based on two key factors: how much impact they’ll have on the user and how feasible they are to implement.
  • How It Works:
    • Draw a 2x2 grid on a paper or board. Label the horizontal axis “Feasibility” (Low to High) and the vertical axis “Impact” (Low to High).
    • Place each idea in the grid based on:
      • Impact: Will this solve the user’s problem effectively? Does it address their core need?
      • Feasibility: Can this realistically be done with your time, skills, and resources?
    • Focus on ideas in the “High Impact, High Feasibility” quadrant (top right). If none are there, consider tweaking a “High Impact, Low Feasibility” idea to make it more doable.
  • Why It’s Useful: It helps balance wild creativity with real-world constraints, ensuring you pick ideas that are both impactful and possible.
  • Example: For gear storage, “portable gear carts” might be High Impact/High Feasibility, while “drones delivering gear” is High Impact/Low Feasibility (but could be adapted to a simpler delivery system).
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Keep it simple—use rough estimates for impact and feasibility. Discuss as a team if you’re unsure where to place an idea.
  • 3. Criteria Scoring – Detailed Evaluation: This method scores ideas against specific criteria to make a more structured decision.
  • How It Works:
    • List 3-5 criteria that matter for your project (e.g., “Meets User Need,” “Cost-Effective,” “Easy to Implement,” “Innovative,” “Scalable”).
    • List your top 5-10 ideas from brainstorming.
    • Score each idea from 1 (low) to 5 (high) for each criterion. Add up the total scores.
    • Choose the highest-scoring ideas (top 1-3) to move forward.
  • Why It’s Useful: It’s more thorough and helps you think critically about what makes an idea “best” for your specific problem. Good for solo work or small teams.
  • Example: For cafeteria lines, “pre-order app” might score high on “Meets User Need” (5) and “Innovative” (4) but lower on “Easy to Implement” (3), totaling 12. Compare to others to decide.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Keep criteria focused on your user and project goals. Don’t overcomplicate—3 criteria might be enough.

Activity for Practice: Take the list of ideas you brainstormed earlier (or create a short list of 5-10 for your problem statement). Pick one of the methods above (Dot Voting, Impact vs. Feasibility Matrix, or Criteria Scoring) and apply it to narrow down to your top 2-3 ideas. If you’re in a group, do this together. Write down which ideas you chose and why the method helped you decide. Share with a classmate or teacher for feedback on your process.

Selecting the best ideas using voting and prioritization methods is the final step of the ideation stage. It helps you transition from a flood of creative possibilities to a focused set of solutions you can prototype and test in the next stage of Design Thinking. As a high schooler, mastering this skill teaches you how to make tough choices and focus on what matters most—a valuable lesson for any challenge you face. You’re ready to take your top ideas and bring them to life!
