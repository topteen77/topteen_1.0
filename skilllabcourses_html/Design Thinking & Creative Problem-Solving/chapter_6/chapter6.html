Chapter 6: Testing – Evaluating and Improving Solutions

 

 

Section 1- Why Testing Matters: Getting Real-World Feedback

Let’s kick things off by understanding why testing matters in the Design Thinking process. Testing is the stage where you put your prototype—whether it’s a sketch, model, or digital mock-up—into the hands of real users to see how it performs in a real-world setting. For high schoolers like you, think of testing as showing your science project at the fair: you’ve built something awesome, but now you need to see if others understand it, like it, and find it useful. This stage isn’t just a final step; it’s a chance to make sure your solution really solves the problem you set out to tackle.

 

The Importance of Testing: Testing is super important because it gives you real-world feedback—honest opinions from the people who will actually use your solution. Up until now, you’ve been imagining how your idea might work, but testing shows you if it actually works. For example, if you made a prototype for a “better school backpack,” testing lets you see if students can carry it comfortably or if the straps break under weight. Without testing, you might miss big flaws or assume something is perfect when it’s not. Testing also builds confidence—if users love your idea, you know you’re on the right track. Plus, it teaches you to listen to others, a skill that’s huge for teamwork in school or future jobs.

 

Key Reasons Testing Matters: Here are some big reasons why testing is a must-do, especially for high school students:

  • Validates Your Solution: Testing checks if your prototype solves the problem from your problem statement (Chapter 3). Does it help your users the way you hoped?
  • Uncovers Hidden Issues: Even the best prototypes can have problems you didn’t notice. Testing with real people helps spot glitches, like a confusing app button or an uncomfortable design.
  • Focuses on Users: Design Thinking is all about the user, and testing keeps their needs at the center. Their feedback tells you what to fix or keep.
  • Saves Time in the Long Run: Finding and fixing issues now prevents bigger headaches later, like if you were to launch a product or pitch an idea that doesn’t work.
  • Encourages Growth: Testing teaches you to handle criticism and learn from mistakes, building resilience for school projects or personal goals.

    •  

How Testing Fits in Design Thinking: Testing comes after Prototyping (Chapter 5) because you need something tangible to evaluate. It’s closely tied to iteration—sometimes testing shows you need to go back and tweak your prototype (or even rethink your idea). For high schoolers, testing can be as simple as showing your prototype to classmates or family and asking for their thoughts. It’s not about fancy labs or expensive tools; it’s about real reactions. And don’t worry—testing isn’t a pass-or-fail grade. Even “bad” feedback is good because it helps you improve.

Example in Action:

  • Problem Statement: “Freshmen need a quicker way to pay for lunch because long lines cut into their break time.”
  • Prototype: A paper mock-up of a cafeteria pre-order app.
  • Why Testing Matters: Showing this prototype to freshmen lets you see if they can figure out how to order and if it feels faster than waiting in line. If they struggle with the layout, you know to fix it before pitching it to the school.

Section 2- Methods for Testing: User Trials, Peer Reviews, and Surveys

Now that you know why testing is so important, let’s explore methods for testing your prototypes to gather that all-important real-world feedback. There are many ways to test, but we’ll focus on three methods that are perfect for high schoolers because they’re simple, doable, and don’t need fancy equipment: User Trials, Peer Reviews, and Surveys. Each method helps you see how your prototype works for others in a different way. Let’s break them down so you can pick the best one (or mix them up) for your project.

 

  • 1. User Trials – Hands-On Testing with Real Users: User trials are when you let the people who will use your solution (your target users) try out your prototype in a real or simulated setting.
  • What It Is: Give your prototype to users and watch them interact with it as if they’re solving the problem. For example, if it’s a new locker organizer, let a classmate pack their books into it.
  • How to Do It: Identify 3-5 users who match your problem statement (e.g., freshmen for a lunch app). Set up a short test session at school or home. Explain the purpose briefly, then observe quietly—don’t help unless they’re stuck. Note what they do, say, or struggle with.
  • Why It’s Useful: You see firsthand how your prototype performs. Do they use it as expected? Where do they get frustrated? It’s great for physical models or apps.
  • Example: For a “cafeteria pre-order app” paper prototype, ask freshmen to “order lunch” by pointing to buttons on the sketch. Watch if they find the “Pay” button easily.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Keep it casual—do trials during lunch or after class. Use your phone to record observations (with permission) if writing is tricky.

    •  

  • 2. Peer Reviews – Feedback from Classmates or Friends: Peer reviews involve showing your prototype to classmates, friends, or family to get their opinions and suggestions.
  • What It Is: Present your prototype to a small group of peers who can act as users or just give fresh perspectives. Ask them to share what they like, dislike, or find confusing.
  • How to Do It: Gather 2-5 peers during a break or study group. Show them your prototype (sketch, model, or digital mock-up) and explain the problem it solves. Let them interact with it or study it, then ask specific questions like “Is this easy to use?” Take notes on their feedback.
  • Why It’s Useful: Peers are easy to access and often honest, giving you quick insights. They might spot things you missed since they’re not as close to the project. It works for all prototype types.
  • Example: Show a cardboard model of a “gear storage cart” to your sports team friends. Ask if it fits their equipment and if it rolls well on the field.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Trade feedback with a friend—review each other’s prototypes to save time. Be open to criticism; it’s not personal!

    •  

  • 3. Surveys – Collecting Written or Online Feedback: Surveys are short questionnaires you give to a larger group to gather opinions about your prototype in a structured way.
  • What It Is: Create a list of 5-10 simple questions about your prototype and ask users or peers to answer them on paper or online. Questions can be yes/no, rating scales, or open-ended.
  • How to Do It: Write your survey using Google Forms (free and easy) or on paper. Share it with 5-10 people who represent your users or just know the problem. Ask about usability, design, or ideas for improvement. Collect and read their answers.
  • Why It’s Useful: Surveys let you get feedback from more people quickly, especially if you can’t meet in person. They’re great for spotting trends (e.g., 8/10 people say it’s too big). Works for any prototype.
  • Example: For a digital mock-up of a “study group app,” make a survey asking, “On a scale of 1-5, how easy is it to join a group?” and share it in a class group chat.
  • Tip for High Schoolers: Keep surveys short (5 minutes max) so people don’t skip them. Use free tools like Google Forms to look professional and save paper.

    •  

Choosing the Right Method: Pick a testing method based on your prototype and resources. If it’s hands-on (like a physical model), try User Trials. If you need quick opinions, use Peer Reviews. If you want lots of input or can’t meet in person, go with Surveys. You can also combine methods—like doing a User Trial with a few people, then sending a Survey to more. The goal is to get honest feedback from the right people.

Section 3- Asking the Right Questions to Gather Useful Insights

You’ve got your testing method ready, but how do you make sure the feedback you get is actually helpful? That’s where asking the right questions comes in. The questions you ask during testing can make a huge difference in understanding if your prototype works for users and how to improve it. For high schoolers, think of this as being a detective—you’re digging for clues about what’s awesome or awful about your idea. In this section, we’ll show you how to craft questions that give you useful insights to make your solution even better.

Why Asking the Right Questions Matters: Good questions help you get clear, honest feedback that points to specific strengths or problems in your prototype. Bad questions—like ones that are too vague or push people to say nice things—can leave you confused or with useless answers. For example, asking “Do you like this?” might just get a “Yeah, it’s fine,” which doesn’t tell you much. But asking “What was hard about using this?” can reveal exact issues to fix. The right questions focus on the user’s experience, not just your feelings, and help you improve during this Testing stage.

 

Tips for Asking Effective Questions: Here are some easy tips to create questions that get you actionable feedback, perfect for high school students:

  • Be Specific: Avoid broad questions like “Is this good?” Instead, target parts of your prototype, like “Was it easy to find the ‘Order’ button on this app mock-up?”
  • Stay Open-Ended: Use questions starting with “What,” “How,” or “Why” to get detailed answers. For example, “What did you think of the size of this backpack model?” lets users explain their thoughts.
  • Focus on the User Experience: Ask about how they felt using it, not just the design. Try “How did it feel to carry this prototype cart?” to learn about comfort or usability.
  • Avoid Leading Questions: Don’t push for positive answers with questions like “Don’t you think this is awesome?” Keep it neutral, like “What’s your opinion on this feature?”
  • Mix Types of Questions: For surveys, include yes/no or rating questions (e.g., “Rate usability from 1-5”) for quick data, plus open-ended ones for deeper insights. In trials or reviews, stick to conversational, open-ended questions.
  • Keep It Short and Simple: Don’t overwhelm testers with too many questions. Pick 3-5 key ones for a quick chat or short survey so they stay engaged.

    •  

Examples of Good Questions by Testing Method: Here are sample questions tailored to each method from the last section, focusing on a “cafeteria pre-order app” prototype:

  • User Trials (During or After Use):
    • “How did you find the process of selecting a meal on this paper mock-up?”
    • “What was the most confusing part of using this prototype?”
    • “What would make ordering faster or easier for you?”
  • Peer Reviews (After Showing Prototype):
    • “What do you think this app screen does, and was it clear to you?”
    • “How would you improve the layout of these buttons?”
    • “Would you use this if it were real? Why or why not?”
  • Surveys (Written or Online):
    • “On a scale of 1-5, how easy was it to understand this app prototype? (1 = Very Hard, 5 = Very Easy)”
    • “What did you like most about this design?”
    • “What one thing would you change to make this better?”

      •  

How to Use Questions During Testing:

  • Before Testing: Write down your 3-5 questions in advance so you don’t forget. Tie them to the key features you want to test (e.g., usability, design, appeal).
  • During Testing: Ask questions naturally if it’s a trial or review. Don’t interrupt too much—let users try the prototype first, then ask. For surveys, make sure instructions are clear.
  • After Testing: Thank testers for their time and honesty. Review their answers to spot patterns or big issues to fix.

    •
