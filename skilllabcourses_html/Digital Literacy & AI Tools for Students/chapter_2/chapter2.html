Chapter 2: Staying Safe Online as a Teen

 

Section 1: Understanding Online Privacy: What High Schoolers Need to Know

 

What is Online Privacy? Online privacy refers to your ability to control who has access to your personal information on the internet. This includes things like your name, address, phone number, location, photos, messages, and even your browsing history. As a high schooler, you might not think twice about sharing a funny story or photo online, but every piece of data you put out there can be seen, stored, or used by others—sometimes without your knowledge. Online privacy is about deciding what to share, who to share it with, and understanding how companies, apps, or even strangers might collect or misuse your information. It’s your right to keep parts of your life private, even in a hyper-connected world.

 

Why Online Privacy Matters for Teens For teens in Grades 9-12, online privacy is crucial because you’re at a stage where your digital actions can have long-term consequences. Sharing too much—like posting your home address in a group chat or tagging your location in a photo—can make you a target for identity theft, stalking, or unwanted attention. Beyond personal safety, companies often track your online behavior (like what you search or like) to sell your data for ads, which can feel invasive. Plus, oversharing now could impact your future; colleges and employers often check applicants’ online profiles, and something as small as an embarrassing post could affect their impression of you. Protecting your privacy helps you stay safe, avoid exploitation, and maintain control over how you’re seen in the digital world.

 

Key Privacy Risks for High Schoolers

  • Data Collection by Apps: Many apps you use (like social media or games) collect data on your habits, location, and preferences, often without clear notice. This data can be sold or leaked.
  • Public Profiles Exposure: If your accounts aren’t set to private, anyone—friends, strangers, or predators—can see your posts, photos, and personal details.
  • Location Tracking: Sharing your location in real-time (like on Snapchat maps) can let others know exactly where you are, which can be dangerous if seen by the wrong person.
  • Hacking and Leaks: Weak security on your accounts can lead to someone accessing your private messages, photos, or other sensitive info, which could be used against you.

    •  

Steps to Protect Your Privacy

  • Read Privacy Policies: Before signing up for an app, skim its privacy policy to see what data it collects and how it’s used (look for simple summaries if the full text is long).
  • Limit What You Share: Avoid posting sensitive info like your full address, school schedule, or family details online, even in “private” messages.
  • Check App Permissions: On your phone, go to settings and see what permissions apps have (e.g., access to camera, location). Turn off anything unnecessary.
  • Use Private Browsing: When researching sensitive topics or shopping, use your browser’s “incognito” mode to avoid leaving a trail of your activity.

    •  

Real-World Example: A Grade 10 student posts a photo of their new house with the caption “Finally moved in!” and tags their exact location. A stranger sees it and starts sending creepy messages. Had they kept their location private, this risk could have been avoided.

Activity: Take 10 minutes to check the privacy settings on one app you use often (like Instagram or Snapchat). Write down 2-3 things you didn’t know about what data it collects or who can see your info. Then, adjust one setting (e.g., turn off location sharing) to make your account more private, and note how it feels to take control of your privacy.

 

 

Section 2: Creating Strong Passwords and Protecting Accounts

Why Strong Passwords Matter Your passwords are the first line of defence for your online accounts—think of them as the lock on your digital front door. As a high schooler, you likely have accounts for social media, school platforms, email, and maybe even online shopping or gaming. If your password is weak or reused across multiple accounts, a hacker could easily guess it, access your personal info, steal your identity, or even lock you out of your own accounts. Strong passwords keep your messages, photos, grades, and money safe from unauthorized access. They’re a simple but powerful way to protect yourself in a world where cybercrime is common.

 

What Makes a Password Strong? A strong password is hard for others to guess but easy for you to remember. Here are the key elements:

  • Length: At least 12-16 characters long (longer is better).
  • Variety: Mix uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and special symbols (like !, @, #).
  • Unpredictability: Avoid obvious choices like “password123,” your name, birthday, or favourite team—hackers try these first.
  • Uniqueness: Use a different password for every account so if one gets hacked, the others stay safe.

    •  

How to Create a Strong Password

  • Use a Passphrase: Turn a memorable sentence into a password by taking the first letter of each word and adding numbers or symbols. Example: “I Love Pizza Every Friday!” becomes “ILPEF!2023”.
  • Randomize It: Use a random mix of characters that aren’t tied to personal info, like “K9m!pL2qW5n@”.
  • Avoid Patterns: Don’t use keyboard patterns (like “qwerty”) or repeating characters (like “1111”).
  • Test It: Some websites or password managers can tell you if your password is strong when you create it.

    •  

Protecting Your Accounts Beyond Passwords

  • Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA): This adds a second step to logins, like entering a code sent to your phone or email after your password. Most social media, email, and school accounts offer this—turn it on!
  • Don’t Share Passwords: Never give your password to anyone, even close friends or family. If you must share access (like for a group project), use temporary codes or shared accounts if possible.
  • Log Out on Shared Devices: If you use a school computer or a friend’s device, always log out of your accounts to prevent others from accessing them.
  • Use a Password Manager: These apps store all your unique passwords securely so you don’t have to remember them all—just one master password. Examples include LastPass or Bitwarden (many are free for basic use).

    •  

Real-World Example: A Grade 11 student uses “Soccer2005” for all their accounts. A hacker guesses it, logs into their email, and resets their social media passwords. If they had used a unique, strong password like “S0cc3r!9kM2pL”, and enabled 2FA, this breach could have been prevented.

Activity: Create a new strong password for one of your accounts using the passphrase method. Write down the sentence you used (not the password itself) and explain why you think it’s secure without revealing the actual password. Then, check if that account offers 2FA in its settings, and enable it if possible. Note down one thing you learned about securing accounts from this exercise.

 

 

Section 3: Recognizing and Avoiding Online Dangers (Cyberbullying, Scams, Catfishing)

What Are Online Dangers? The internet can expose high schoolers to risks that threaten your emotional, financial, or personal safety. Three common dangers for teens are cyberbullying, scams, and catfishing. Cyberbullying is when someone uses digital platforms to harass, threaten, or humiliate you, often through mean comments, rumors, or fake profiles. Scams are tricks designed to steal your money or info, like fake emails asking for your password or “win a prize” links. Catfishing is when someone pretends to be someone they’re not online (like using fake photos) to build trust and manipulate you, often for emotional or harmful reasons. Knowing how to spot and avoid these dangers is essential to staying safe as a teen online.

 

Recognizing Cyberbullying

  • Signs: Repeated hurtful messages, public shaming posts, or threats on social media, texts, or gaming chats. It might include fake accounts mocking you or spreading lies.
  • What to Do: Don’t respond or retaliate—it can escalate. Screenshot the evidence, block the person, report it to the platform, and tell a trusted adult (parent, teacher, counselor). Keep records in case it gets worse.
  • Prevention: Keep your profiles private, avoid sharing personal struggles online, and think before commenting—don’t accidentally contribute to bullying others.

    •  

Spotting Scams and Phishing

  • Signs: Emails, texts, or pop-ups that urge you to click a link, enter personal info, or pay money quickly. They often mimic trusted brands (like “Apple Support”) but have weird spelling, bad grammar, or suspicious URLs (check before clicking).
  • What to Do: Don’t click or reply. Delete the message, report it as spam, and verify directly with the real company if it claims to be them (use their official website, not the message’s link).
  • Prevention: Never share passwords or credit card info online unless you’re 100% sure of the site. Use antivirus software on your devices to flag risky links.

    •  

Identifying Catfishing

  • Signs: Someone online refuses to video chat or meet in person, their photos look too perfect (like stock images), or their stories don’t add up (e.g., claiming to be a teen but using outdated slang). They might push for personal info or money.
  • What to Do: Stop sharing personal details, reverse-search their photos on Google to check if they’re stolen, and cut contact if you’re suspicious. Tell a trusted adult if they pressure you.
  • Prevention: Don’t accept friend requests from strangers, keep chats casual until you’re sure of someone’s identity, and trust your gut if something feels off.

    •  

Real-World Example: A Grade 9 student gets a message from a “friend” on Instagram asking for their login to “help with something.” It’s a scam, and their account gets hacked. If they had questioned the odd request and checked the profile for red flags (like no mutual friends), they could have avoided the trap.

Activity: Look up a recent news story or watch a short video about a teen facing one of these online dangers (cyberbullying, scam, or catfishing). Write a brief summary (3-5 sentences) of what happened, how they could have recognized the danger earlier, and one tip from this section that could have helped them. Share your findings with a classmate or family member to raise awareness.

 

 

Section 4: Safe Use of Social Media (Privacy Settings, Posting Wisely)

Why Social Media Safety Matters for Teens Social media is a huge part of high school life—apps like Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, and Twitter are where you connect, express yourself, and follow trends. But as a teen, using social media without caution can lead to privacy breaches, cyberbullying, or content that haunts you later. Every post, story, or comment is part of your online identity, and it’s easy to overshare without realizing who’s watching. Safe social media use means controlling your privacy settings and thinking carefully about what you post to protect your safety, relationships, and reputation. It’s about enjoying these platforms without unnecessary risks.

 

Mastering Privacy Settings

  • Set Profiles to Private: Most apps let you make your account private so only approved followers see your content. On Instagram, go to Settings &gt; Privacy &gt; Private Account. Do this for every platform you use.
  • Control Who Sees What: Adjust who can view specific posts or stories (e.g., “Close Friends” on Instagram) and disable location tagging to hide where you are.
  • Limit Data Sharing: Turn off options that let the app access your contacts, location, or other apps unless needed. Check these in the app’s settings under “Privacy” or “Security.”
  • Block and Report: If someone bothers you, block them to stop contact and report inappropriate behavior to the platform. Don’t hesitate to use these tools.

    •  

Posting Wisely: Think Before You Share

  • Ask Yourself Questions: Before posting, ask: “Could this embarrass me later? Could it be misunderstood? Would I want a teacher or employer to see this?” If the answer is no, reconsider.
  • Avoid Sensitive Info: Don’t share your address, phone number, school location, or family details, even in captions or DMs. Hackers or creeps can use this.
  • Be Kind and Respectful: Avoid posting mean comments or content that could hurt others—cyberbullying can backfire on you emotionally or legally.
  • Remember Permanence: Even “temporary” posts (like Snapchat stories) can be screenshot or saved. Assume anything online can last forever.

    •  

Real-World Example: A Grade 12 student posts a rant about their teacher on a public Twitter account, forgetting their profile isn’t private. The teacher sees it, and it creates awkward tension at school. Setting their account to private and thinking twice about venting online could have prevented this.

Activity: Open one social media app you use most. Spend 10 minutes exploring its privacy settings (look under “Settings” or “Account”). Write down 2-3 settings you can adjust to make your account safer (e.g., private mode, disable location). Then, think of a recent post you made—did it follow the “think before you share” tips? If not, note one way you’ll be more careful next time.

 

 

Section 5: Managing Your Digital Footprint for College and Job Applications

What is a Digital Footprint? Your digital footprint is the trail of data you leave behind online through everything you post, like, comment on, or search. For high schoolers, this includes social media posts, photos, forum comments, gaming profiles, and even old accounts you’ve forgotten about. There are two types: active (things you intentionally share, like a TikTok video) and passive (data collected about you, like websites you visit). As a teen, your digital footprint is already forming, and it can shape how colleges, scholarship committees, and future employers see you. Managing it means ensuring this trail reflects the best version of yourself.

 

Why It Matters for Your Future Colleges and employers often search applicants online to learn more about them. A positive digital footprint—think thoughtful posts, creative projects, or volunteer photos—can impress them and show you’re responsible. But a negative one, like inappropriate jokes, rude comments, or party photos, can hurt your chances, even if you’re qualified. Studies show over 70% of employers check social media during hiring, and many colleges do too. As a high schooler, now is the time to clean up or build a footprint that helps, not harms, your goals for after graduation.

 

Steps to Manage Your Digital Footprint

  • Google Yourself: Search your name (in quotes, like “John Smith”) to see what comes up. Look at images, posts, or old accounts. If you find something negative, try to delete it or make it private.
  • Clean Up Old Content: Go through your social media accounts and delete or hide posts, photos, or comments that could be seen as unprofessional or embarrassing. Even “private” posts can leak.
  • Build a Positive Presence: Share content that shows your skills or character, like a school project on LinkedIn, volunteer work on Instagram, or a respectful opinion on Twitter. Be someone you’d want to admit or hire.
  • Lock Down Accounts: Make personal accounts private, and create separate public profiles (if needed) for professional purposes, like a portfolio for college apps.
  • Think Long-Term: Before posting anything, ask, “How would this look to a college admissions officer or boss in 5 years?” If it’s risky, skip it.

    •  

Real-World Example: A Grade 11 student applies for a scholarship, but the committee finds old Facebook posts with rude language from middle school. They’re passed over, even with great grades. If they had Googled themselves earlier and deleted those posts, their application might have stood out positively.

Activity: Spend 15 minutes searching your name online (use Google or another search engine). Write down 2-3 things you found about yourself (good or bad) and note if they’re public or private. Then, pick one social media account and delete or hide one old post that might not look good to a college or employer. Finally, brainstorm one positive thing you could post this week (like a school achievement) to build a better digital footprint, and share your plan with a friend or family member for feedback.
