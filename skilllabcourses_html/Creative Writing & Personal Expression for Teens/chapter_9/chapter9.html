Chapter 9: Sharing Your Voice with the World

 

Section 1: Platforms for Publishing Teen Writing (Blogs, School Magazines, Online Forums)

What’s This About? You’ve written something dope—maybe a poem about late-night thoughts or a story about a weird school day. Now, where do you share it? There are tons of platforms made for teen writers to publish their work, from school mags to online spaces where people vibe with your style. Publishing isn’t just for “famous” writers; it’s for you, right now, to start building your name and connecting with readers who get you.

Why It Matters for Teens: As a high schooler, getting your work out there can boost your confidence, help you find friends who love writing too, and even impress colleges or future bosses. Plus, seeing your name in print (or online) feels like a total win. Let’s explore three main platforms where you can start sharing today.

Key Platforms to Try:

  • Blogs (Your Own or Group Ones):
    • What It Is: A blog is like your personal online diary or story hub. You can start one for free on sites like WordPress, Blogger, or Medium. Write about anything—your latest short story, rants about life, or even reviews of games or shows.
    • Why It’s Cool for Teens: You’re the boss! You control what you post, how often, and the vibe. Plus, you can build a following of peeps who dig your style.
    • Example: Imagine you’re into sci-fi. You start a blog called “Alien Teen Diaries” and post a new story every week about a high schooler on Mars. One post gets shared by a classmate on TikTok, and suddenly, 50 people are reading your stuff. That’s the power of blogging!
    • Pro Tip: Use fun visuals (like memes or Canva graphics) to make your blog pop. And don’t stress about perfection—teens love raw, real content. Start with a short post, like 300 words, and see how it feels.
    • Try This: Pick a topic you’re obsessed with (skateboarding, anime, whatever) and write a 1-paragraph blog post. Share it with a friend to test the waters.
  • School Magazines or Newspapers:
    • What It Is: Most high schools have a lit mag, yearbook, or newspaper where students submit poems, essays, stories, or even art. It’s a built-in audience of your peers and teachers.
    • Why It’s Cool for Teens: It’s a safe first step to publish—your school community already knows you, and it looks awesome on college apps. Plus, you might get printed copies to show off to fam.
    • Example: Say your school mag, “The Wildcat Word,” has a theme of “Hidden Talents.” You submit a funny poem about how you’re secretly a pro at karaoke. It gets picked, and at the next assembly, people are quoting your lines. Instant fame in the cafeteria!
    • Pro Tip: Check submission rules (like word count or deadlines) and ask your English teacher for help polishing your piece. Make it personal—write about something only you could tell.
    • Try This: Find out if your school has a mag or paper (ask a teacher or check the library). Brainstorm one idea for a piece you could submit this semester.
  • Online Forums and Communities:
    • What It Is: Sites like Wattpad, Reddit (subreddits like r/Writing or r/TeenWriters), and Teen Ink are spaces where young writers share stories, get feedback, and chat with others. It’s like a giant writing club online.
    • Why It’s Cool for Teens: You connect with writers your age from everywhere, not just your town. You can post anonymously if you’re shy, and some platforms let you enter contests or get “upvotes” for clout.
    • Example: On Wattpad, you post a romance story about two gamers falling in love over Discord. A reader comments, “This is SO relatable!” and soon, you’ve got 100 reads and a few followers hyping you up.
    • Pro Tip: Pick a platform that matches your vibe (Wattpad for stories, Teen Ink for serious poems). Read other teens’ work first to get a feel for the community, and always be kind in comments—online rep matters.
    • Try This: Sign up for one forum (like Wattpad) and post a short piece or just comment on someone else’s work to dip your toe in.

Fun Twist for Teens: Turn publishing into a squad goal! Team up with friends to start a group blog or submit to the school mag together. Make it a challenge—who can get the most reads or comments by the end of the month? Loser buys pizza. Sharing your voice is more fun with your crew hyping you up.

 

Section 2: Participating in Writing Contests and Workshops

What’s This About? Contests and workshops are like the ultimate level-up for teen writers. Contests let you test your skills against others and win prizes (or just bragging rights), while workshops are chill spaces to learn new tricks, meet writers, and get feedback. Both push you out of your comfort zone in the best way.

Why It Matters for Teens: As a high schooler, these opportunities can teach you deadlines, how to handle pressure, and tricks pros use. Winning a contest or finishing a workshop also looks fire on resumes or college apps. Plus, you might discover you’re better than you thought!

Breaking It Down:

  • Writing Contests:
    • What They Are: Competitions where you submit a piece (story, poem, essay) based on a theme or prompt. Some are local (school or library), others national or online (like Scholastic Art &amp; Writing Awards or YoungArts). Prizes can be cash, publication, or swag.
    • Why It’s Cool for Teens: It’s a chance to shine! Even if you don’t win, judges often give feedback, and it’s a thrill to say, “I entered a real contest.”
    • Example: You enter a local library contest with the theme “Future Dreams.” Your story about a teen inventing a time-travel app wins third place, and you get $25 plus a shout-out at a library event. Your friends are shook—suddenly, you’re “that writer kid.”
    • Pro Tip: Start small with school or local contests to build confidence. Read past winners’ work (often online) to see what stands out, and follow the rules exactly (word count, format) so you don’t get disqualified.
    • Where to Find Them: Check your school bulletin, library boards, or online at sites like Reedsy or WritingContest.com. Ask teachers—they often know hidden gems.
    • Try This: Search for one teen contest online or at school. Write down the deadline and theme, and brainstorm a quick idea for it.
  • Writing Workshops:
    • What They Are: Group sessions (in-person or online) where you learn writing skills, do exercises, and share work for feedback. Some are free (libraries, schools), others cost a bit (summer camps or online courses like Coursera).
    • Why It’s Cool for Teens: You meet other writers your age, learn hacks (like how to write killer dialogue), and get comfy sharing. It’s like a writing party with less judgment.
    • Example: You join a free weekend workshop at your community center. The leader teaches “show, don’t tell,” and you rewrite a boring line like “I was sad” into “My chest felt like a deflated balloon.” Everyone claps for your edit, and you leave with two new writer friends.
    • Pro Tip: Look for teen-specific workshops—they’re less intimidating. Bring a notebook for ideas, and don’t be afraid to ask questions or mess up. It’s all about learning.
    • Where to Find Them: Check school clubs, local libraries, or online platforms like Hugo House or GrubStreet. Some are Zoom-based, so you can join from your room.
    • Try This: Ask a teacher or librarian about a nearby workshop, or search “teen writing workshop near me.” Sign up for one (or just watch a free YouTube writing vid to start).

Fun Twist for Teens: Make contests a game! Bet with a friend on who can enter more contests this year, or host a mini-workshop with your squad—take turns teaching a trick (like funny metaphors) and roast each other’s drafts in a nice way. Add snacks, and it’s a vibe.

 

Section 3: Building an Online Portfolio of Your Work

What’s This About? An online portfolio is like your personal highlight reel—a website or page showing off your best writing. It’s a spot to collect your stories, poems, or essays so anyone (friends, colleges, even future publishers) can see what you’ve got. Think of it as your writer Instagram, but pro-level.

Why It Matters for Teens: Having a portfolio screams, “I’m serious about writing!” It’s perfect for sharing with teachers for extra credit, applying to contests, or just flexing your skills. Plus, in a world where everyone’s online, it’s how you stand out as a teen creative.

How to Build One (Easy Steps):

  • Pick a Platform:
    • Use free sites like Wix, Weebly, or Google Sites to make a simple webpage. Or, if you’re on socials, a pinned post on Instagram or a Twitter thread can work as a mini-portfolio.
    • Example: You set up a Wix site called “Jay’s Jottings.” It’s got a black-and-red theme (your fave colors) and a homepage saying, “Yo, I’m Jay, a teen writer with wild stories.” Instant cool factor.
  • Choose Your Best Work:
    • Pick 3-5 pieces that show your range—like a funny story, a deep poem, and a random rant. Update it every few months with new stuff.
    • Example: You upload your viral Wattpad story, a poem from English class that got an A, and a blog post about your dog’s weird habits. Variety shows you’re not a one-trick pony.
  • Add Personality:
    • Write a short “About Me” (like “I’m a 16-year-old who writes to escape math class”) and toss in a pic or meme if it’s your vibe. Keep it short but real.
    • Example: Your “About Me” says, “I turn coffee and chaos into stories. Hit me up if you love dystopia or dogs.” Teens reading it will vibe instantly.
  • Share It Smart:
    • Link it in your social bios, email sig, or even print it on a QR code for school projects. Don’t spam, but don’t hide it either.
    • Pro Tip: Keep it clean—no typos or weird formatting. Ask a friend to click through and make sure it looks good on phone and laptop.

Why It’s Cool for Teens: A portfolio isn’t just “work”—it’s your brand. Imagine a college admissions officer seeing your site and thinking, “This kid’s got passion.” Or a contest judge checking it out and picking you ‘cause your style stands out. It’s your digital flex.

Fun Twist for Teens: Turn portfolio-building into a glow-up challenge. Spend a weekend making it with a playlist blasting, and add Easter eggs (like a hidden joke in the footer). Show it to your bestie and see if they can find the secret. Make it YOU.

Try This: Pick a free site (like Wix) and set up a basic page with your name and one piece of writing. Takes 30 minutes tops. Screenshot it and text it to a friend for hype.

 

Section 4: Handling Criticism and Growing as a Writer

What’s This About? Sharing your writing means people will react—some will love it, some will hate it, and some will straight-up critique it. Criticism (even the harsh kind) isn’t a diss; it’s a tool to make you better. This section teaches you how to take feedback, not take it personally, and use it to grow as a teen writer.

Why It Matters for Teens: High school is full of opinions, and writing is super personal. Learning to handle criticism now means you won’t crumble when a teacher, reader, or troll says something tough. It’s also how you turn “meh” work into “whoa” work—feedback is your cheat code.

How to Deal with Criticism Like a Pro:

  • Listen Without Flipping Out:
    • When someone critiques your work, don’t argue or cry (even if you wanna). Take a deep breath and just hear them out. They’re not attacking YOU, just the words on the page.
    • Example: Your friend reads your story and says, “The ending felt rushed.” You wanna snap, “You don’t get it!” but instead, you nod and ask, “What felt off about it?” Turns out, they wanted more drama in the last scene—solid idea!
    • Pro Tip: Write down feedback so you don’t forget it, even if it stings at first.
  • Sort the Helpful from the Hate:
    • Not all criticism is gold. If someone says, “This sucks,” with no reason, ignore it—they’re just venting. But if they say, “Your character felt flat ‘cause they didn’t react to the breakup,” that’s specific and useful.
    • Example: On Wattpad, a random commenter types, “Boring af.” Meh, skip. But another says, “I didn’t get why the hero gave up—maybe add their thoughts?” That’s worth thinking about.
    • Pro Tip: Look for patterns—if three people say your dialogue’s stiff, they’re probably right. One troll? Brush it off.
  • Use It to Grow:
    • Pick one or two critique points to fix in your next draft or piece. Don’t try to change everything, or you’ll lose your voice. Growth is slow, not a total makeover.
    • Example: After that “rushed ending” feedback, you add two paragraphs of your main character panicking before the final choice. Rereading it, you’re like, “Dang, this hits harder now.” Growth unlocked!
    • Pro Tip: Keep old drafts to compare—seeing how far you’ve come is a mood boost.
  • Don’t Fear the Haters:
    • Some criticism (especially online) is just people being mean. Remember, your writing isn’t for everyone, and that’s okay. Keep your squad close for support when it gets rough.
    • Example: A Reddit user trashes your poem with “This is cringe.” Ouch, but your bestie texts, “Bro, I cried reading it—it’s real.” Focus on the love, not the shade.

Why It’s Cool for Teens: Handling criticism makes you tough—not just in writing, but in life. It’s like learning to shrug off a bad grade or a dumb rumor. Plus, every big writer (even TikTok poets or YA authors) got roasted at some point. It’s your rite of passage.

Fun Twist for Teens: Turn feedback into a game. Next time you get a critique, rate it 1-5 on “Ouch Factor” (how much it hurt) and “Usefulness” (how much it helps). Then, challenge yourself to fix one thing from it within a week. Reward? Treat yourself to boba or an extra Netflix ep. Growth can be fun!

Try This: Share a short piece with a friend or family member and ask for one thing they’d change. Write down their feedback (no arguing!) and decide if you’ll use it. Reflect on how it felt to hear their thoughts.

 

 

Chapter Wrap-up

Sharing your writing is a big deal, teen writer. The world’s waiting to hear what only you can say. So, grab your laptop or pen, pick one idea from this chapter, and share something this week. You’ve got this!
