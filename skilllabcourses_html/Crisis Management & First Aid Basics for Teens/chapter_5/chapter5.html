Chapter 5: Responding to Major Medical Emergencies

 

Section 1: Choking

What’s Choking All About? Choking is when something—like food, gum, or a small object—gets stuck in someone’s airway, blocking their ability to breathe. For teens, this can sneak up during a rushed lunch, while scarfing down snacks at a game, or even messing around with friends. Younger kids you might babysit are at risk too, especially with candy or toys. It’s a silent but deadly situation because the person often can’t yell for help. The good news? You can be the difference between a scary moment and a tragedy by acting fast. Let’s break down how to spot it and save the day.

Recognizing Choking Signs Not every cough means choking—sometimes it’s just a crumb gone wrong. But real choking is an emergency, and you’ve gotta know the signs to jump in:

  • Can’t Speak or Make Noise: They might try to talk but only wheeze or stay silent. Often, they’ll point to their throat with a panicked look.
  • Weak or Silent Cough: If they’re coughing but it’s super weak or not clearing anything, their airway is blocked.
  • Blue Lips or Face: This is a huge red flag—lack of oxygen turns skin bluish or grayish, especially around the mouth.
  • Clutching Their Throat: This is the universal choking signal, like something out of a movie. If you see this, don’t wait—act!
  • Flailing or Panic: They might wave their arms, grab at their neck, or look terrified as they struggle to breathe.

Teen Scenario: Picture this—you’re at a school dance, and your friend Mia is munching on hard candy. Suddenly, she stops dancing, grabs her throat, and her face starts turning red. She can’t say a word and looks freaked out. That’s choking, and you’re up to bat. No time to freeze—let’s learn what to do next.

Performing the Heimlich Manoeuvre (for Teens and Adults) The Heimlich manoeuvre is your secret weapon against choking. It’s a quick, forceful move to push air from the lungs and pop out whatever’s stuck. Think of it as a life-saving bear hug. Here’s the step-by-step for someone your age or older:

  • Confirm They’re Choking: Ask, “Are you choking?” If they can’t answer but nod or signal “yes,” move fast. If they can talk or cough hard, let them try to clear it themselves first.
  • Get Behind Them: Stand directly behind the person, staying steady on your feet.
  • Wrap Your Arms: Reach around their waist like you’re about to lift them.
  • Make a Fist: Place one hand in a fist just above their belly button (navel)—not on the ribs, but in the soft spot below the chest bone. Keep your thumb side pressed against their stomach.
  • Grab Your Fist: Use your other hand to cover your fist for extra power.
  • Thrust Up and In: Pull sharply inward and upward, like you’re trying to scoop something out. Use quick, strong thrusts—don’t be shy; it needs force! Repeat until the object flies out (you might see food or hear them gasp) or they can breathe again.
  • Aftercare: Once they’re breathing, they might cough or feel sore. Encourage them to sit down and sip water. If they’re still struggling or something feels off, get them to a doctor ASAP—sometimes bits can linger in the airway.

For Younger Children (Like a Little Sibling or Kid You’re Babysitting) If you’re watching a younger kid—say, under 8 years old or much smaller than you—the Heimlich needs a gentler touch. Their bodies are more fragile, so adjust like this:

  • Get to Their Level: Kneel down so you’re not towering over them, or pick them up if they’re super small.
  • Position Carefully: Place your fist above their navel, just like with adults, but don’t squeeze as hard.
  • Gentle Thrusts: Use less force—think firm but not crushing. Do quick upward thrusts until the item comes out.
  • For Babies (Under 1 Year): Skip the Heimlich entirely—it’s too risky. Instead, lay them face-down on your forearm (support their head slightly lower than their chest), and give 5 firm back blows between their shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. If that doesn’t work, flip them face-up and do 5 chest thrusts (push with two fingers in the center of their chest, just below the nipple line). Repeat until it clears or help arrives.

Real-Life Teen Example: You’re babysitting your 6-year-old cousin at a birthday party, and he chokes on a grape. He’s coughing weakly and turning blue. You kneel down, wrap your arms around his tiny frame, and give gentle Heimlich thrusts. Pop! The grape flies out, and he’s crying but breathing. You’re basically a superhero now—nice work!

What If You’re Choking Alone? This is rare but scary. If no one’s around, don’t panic—you can save yourself with a self-Heimlich:

  • Make a fist and place it just above your navel.
  • Lean over a hard surface like a chair back, table edge, or counter.
  • Push your fist into your stomach by pressing hard against the surface. Keep thrusting until the object dislodges or help shows up.
  • Bonus: If you can, call 911 first and leave the phone on speaker so they know you’re in trouble.

Fun Teen Activity: Grab your squad and practice the Heimlich on a big stuffed animal or a rolled-up sleeping bag. Set a timer—who can “save” their teddy the fastest? Laugh about it, but remember the moves. Bonus points if you film a funny TikTok of your “rescue mission” (just don’t actually choke anyone, duh!).

Cool Fact for Teens: Did you know the Heimlich maneuver was invented by Dr. Henry Heimlich in 1974? He even used it himself at 96 years old to save someone in his nursing home. Talk about a lifelong hero!

 

Section 2: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) Basics

Why CPR Is a Game-Changer CPR, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation, is the ultimate lifesaver when someone’s heart stops beating or they’re not breathing. This can happen during a heart attack, drowning, or even a random collapse. As teens, you don’t need to be paramedics to make a difference—learning hands-only CPR can keep someone alive until the pros get there. Think of yourself as the critical link in the “chain of survival.” Every second counts, and you pumping their chest could be what keeps their brain and heart going. Let’s get into it!

When to Perform CPR CPR isn’t for every situation—only use it when someone is completely unresponsive and not breathing normally. Here’s how to figure it out:

  • Check Responsiveness: Tap their shoulders hard or shake them a bit while shouting, “Hey, are you okay? Can you hear me?” No reaction at all? Keep going.
  • Look for Breathing: Lean close and watch their chest for 5-10 seconds. Is it moving up and down? If not, or if they’re only making weird gasping sounds (called “agonal breathing”), they’re in trouble.
  • Skip the Pulse Check: Don’t bother feeling for a pulse—it’s hard to find even for trained folks, and wasting time can cost a life. If they’re out cold and not breathing right, assume the worst and start CPR.
  • Exceptions: If they’re just asleep or drunk but breathing okay, don’t do CPR. But if there’s any doubt, err on the side of action.

How to Do Hands-Only CPR (Perfect for Teens) Hands-only CPR is a simplified version that skips mouth-to-mouth (no awkwardness here!). It’s all about chest compressions to mimic the heart’s pumping. Follow these steps:

  • Call for Help Immediately: Before anything, yell for someone nearby to call 911. If you’re alone, dial it yourself, put the phone on speaker, and tell them, “I’ve got someone down, not breathing, starting CPR at [location].” Don’t hang up—dispatchers might guide you.
  • Set Up: Get the person on a hard, flat surface (floor or ground, not a soft bed or couch—compressions won’t work there). Kneel next to their chest, facing their torso.
  • Find the Spot: Place the heel of one hand in the center of their chest, right between the nipples (on the breastbone). Stack your other hand on top, fingers interlaced or overlapping.
  • Push Hard and Fast: Lock your elbows straight, keep your shoulders over your hands, and push down hard—about 2 inches deep (or deeper for bigger adults). It might feel like you’re breaking ribs (that’s normal, don’t stop). Go fast, at a rate of 100-120 compressions per minute. Pro tip: Sing “Stayin’ Alive” by the Bee Gees in your head—the beat matches perfectly!
  • Don’t Stop: Keep pushing until paramedics arrive, someone else takes over, or you’re physically exhausted. If you’re tired, switch with a friend if possible—teamwork saves lives. Don’t worry about perfect form; doing something is better than nothing.
  • If They Wake Up: If they start breathing or moving, stop CPR but stay close. Roll them onto their side (recovery position) if they’re not fully awake, and watch until help comes.

Teen Scenario: Imagine you’re at a skate park, and an older dude watching the tricks suddenly collapses on a bench. You run over, tap him, yell—he’s out cold, no chest movement. You shout for a friend to call 911, drop to your knees, and start pumping his chest to the beat of your favourite hype song. People are staring, but you’re keeping his blood flowing until the ambulance rolls up. You’re a total legend!

Importance of Calling Emergency Services CPR isn’t a permanent fix—it’s a stall tactic to keep oxygen moving until medics bring the big guns (like defibrillators). Always call 911 or have someone do it the second you realize there’s a problem. Give clear info: exact location (like “west side of Lincoln High parking lot”), what’s happening (“person collapsed, not breathing”), and that CPR is underway. If you’re on speaker, the dispatcher might count compressions with you or give tips. Never assume “someone else will call”—take charge!

What If You’re Not Sure You’re Doing It Right? Don’t stress about perfection. Studies show even messy CPR doubles survival chances. If you’re pushing hard and fast, you’re helping. Ignore weird sounds (like rib cracks or gasps)—it’s normal and means you’re doing real work. Just keep going until help arrives.

Fun Teen Challenge: Grab your crew and download a free CPR training app (like the American Heart Association’s) or watch a hands-only CPR video on YouTube. Practice compressions on a firm pillow or rolled-up blanket—time each other to “Stayin’ Alive” or another 120 BPM banger like “Sweet Child O’ Mine.” Make it a game: Who can keep the rhythm longest? Bonus if you post a group “CPR practice” story on Insta to hype up your friends!

Cool Fact for Teens: Hands-only CPR became a thing because people were grossed out by mouth-to-mouth. Since 2010, it’s been the go-to for untrained folks like us—and it works just as well for most sudden cardiac arrests. You’re basically a heart-pumping DJ!

 

Section 3: Seizures and Fainting

What’s the Deal with Seizures and Fainting? Seizures and fainting can look terrifying, but they’re often manageable with the right moves. A seizure is like a short-circuit in the brain, causing uncontrolled shaking, staring, or weird movements—sometimes from epilepsy, sometimes from injury or fever. Fainting (or “syncope”) is when someone passes out briefly, usually from low blood pressure or dehydration, like standing too long in the heat. As teens, you might see this during stressful finals week, at packed concerts, or on the sports field. Your job isn’t to “fix” it but to keep them safe and calm until it’s over. Let’s dive in with detailed steps.

What to Do During a Seizure Seeing someone have a seizure—like full-body shaking or zoning out—can be freaky, but don’t panic. They’re not in pain, and most seizures stop on their own in a few minutes. Here’s how to be their protector:

  • Stay Calm and Clear Space: First, don’t scream or freak out—your vibe sets the tone. Move desks, chairs, backpacks, or anything hard/sharp away from them so they don’t bang into stuff while shaking. If they’re near stairs or a ledge, block it off if you can.
  • Protect Their Head: If they’re on the ground, slide something soft under their head—like a hoodie, backpack, or even your hands (if nothing else is around). Don’t try to hold their body still—it won’t stop the seizure and could hurt them.
  • Don’t Put Anything in Their Mouth: Old-school myths say to stick a spoon or wallet in there to “stop biting.” Nope! That’s dangerous—they could choke or break teeth. Just let the seizure run its course.
  • Time the Seizure: Check your phone or watch—how long does it last? Most are 1-3 minutes. If it’s over 5 minutes, or they have back-to-back seizures without waking up, that’s an emergency.
  • Post-Seizure Care: When it stops, they’ll likely be dazed, tired, or confused. Roll them gently onto their side (recovery position—knees bent, one arm under head) to keep their airway clear if they vomit. Speak softly, like, “Hey, you’re okay, I’m here,” and don’t let them stand up too fast. Stay until they’re fully back or help arrives.
  • When to Call 911: Call if it’s their first seizure ever, lasts over 5 minutes, they’re injured (like a head hit), they’re pregnant, or they don’t wake up after. If they’re known to have epilepsy and it’s a “normal” seizure for them, ask if they want help afterward.

Teen Scenario: You’re in biology class, and your lab partner, Jay, suddenly drops to the floor, shaking hard. You push desks away, grab your jacket to cushion his head, and tell someone to get the teacher. It lasts 2 minutes, then he’s groggy. You roll him on his side, chill with him, and let the nurse know. Everyone’s calling you a rockstar for staying cool under pressure!

Helping Someone Who Has Fainted Fainting is less dramatic than a seizure—someone just collapses and usually wakes up quick (under a minute). It’s often from heat, hunger, stress, or standing too long. Here’s how to handle it like a pro:

  • Catch or Lay Them Down: If you see them wobbling, try to guide them to the ground safely so they don’t crack their head. If they’re already down, keep them flat on their back—don’t prop them up yet. Lying flat gets blood back to their brain.
  • Elevate Their Legs: If there’s no injury (like a twisted ankle from falling), lift their legs about 12-18 inches off the ground—rest them on a chair, backpack, or your lap. Hold for a few minutes to boost blood flow.
  • Check Breathing and Response: Make sure their chest is rising/falling. Tap them and say, “Hey, can you hear me?” If they don’t wake up in 1-2 minutes or aren’t breathing, switch to CPR mode (see Section 2) and call 911.
  • When They Wake Up: They’ll probably be confused or embarrassed. Keep them lying down for a bit—don’t let them jump up or they might faint again. Offer water if they’re alert, and ask if they feel okay. If they’re dizzy, pale, or it’s not their first faint, get a teacher or call for medical help.
  • Look for Causes: Ask, “Did you eat today? Were you super hot or stressed?” Fainting can signal low blood sugar or dehydration, so nudge them to snack or hydrate after.

Teen Scenario: At a scorching hot football game, your friend Zoe sways and faints in the bleachers. You help her lie down, prop her legs on a cooler, and fan her with a program while someone grabs water. She wakes up in 30 seconds, super embarrassed, but you’re like, “No biggie, I got you.” She’s fine after sipping Gatorade—total win!

Teen Self-Care Tip: Ever feel lightheaded during a long assembly or after skipping breakfast? Sit down fast or put your head between your knees to rush blood back to your brain. Tell your crew this hack—y’all can avoid fainting fails together!

Cool Fact for Teens: Fainting is sometimes called a “swoon” in old books—think dramatic movie scenes. It’s your body’s reset button when blood pressure drops. And seizures? They’ve been around forever—even ancient Romans wrote about them. You’re learning history and hero skills at once!

Group Activity: Role-play with friends—one pretends to faint during a “fake concert” in your backyard. Practice laying them down and elevating legs while others “call for help.” Make it funny but real—add crowd noise for extra chaos!

 

Section 4: Allergic Reactions and Anaphylaxis

What’s the Big Deal with Anaphylaxis? Allergies are common—maybe you sneeze from cats or get itchy from pollen. But anaphylaxis is the extreme, life-threatening version of an allergic reaction. It can strike within minutes from triggers like peanuts, shellfish, bee stings, latex, or certain meds. For teens, this might happen at a cafeteria lunch with hidden ingredients, a camping trip with wasps, or a party with surprise snacks. It shuts down the body fast—breathing gets hard, swelling explodes, and without help, it can be fatal. But you’ve got the power to spot it and act. Let’s get detailed!

Identifying Severe Allergic Reactions (Anaphylaxis) Mild allergies might mean a rash or sniffles, but anaphylaxis is a full-body emergency. It often hits with multiple symptoms at once—watch for these:

  • Swelling Gone Wild: Lips, tongue, throat, or face puff up fast. They might say, “My throat feels tight,” or struggle to swallow/speak.
  • Breathing Trouble: Wheezing, shortness of breath, coughing, or a feeling like their chest is closing in. This is the airway swelling shut—super dangerous.
  • Skin Reactions: Hives (red, itchy bumps), widespread redness, or intense itching all over. Their skin might look like a bad sunburn in seconds.
  • Gut Issues: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, or stomach cramps, often paired with other signs.
  • Feeling of Doom: They might say, “I feel like I’m gonna die,” or seem super anxious. This weird dread is a real symptom!
  • Dizziness or Fainting: Blood pressure drops, making them lightheaded or pass out. Key Note: If you see 2 or more of these after a known trigger (like eating nuts), assume anaphylaxis, even if it’s mild at first—it can worsen fast.

Teen Scenario: You’re at a school bake sale, and your buddy Sam takes a bite of a brownie. Two minutes later, his lips swell, he’s wheezing, and scratching his arms like crazy. He mumbles, “I think there’s nuts in this,” and looks terrified. That’s anaphylaxis—don’t wait to see if it gets “worse.” Time to move!

Using an EpiPen (If Available) and Seeking Help An EpiPen (or generic auto-injector) is a portable shot of epinephrine, a drug that can reverse anaphylaxis temporarily by opening airways and boosting blood pressure. Many people with severe allergies carry one. If it’s around, here’s how to use it:

  • Locate the EpiPen: Ask, “Do you have an EpiPen?” if they can talk. If not, check their bag, pocket, or ask a friend—most are in a labelled case. Some schools keep spares in the nurse’s office.
  • Prepare It: Pull off the safety cap (often blue or gray—read the label if you’re unsure). Don’t touch the other end (orange or black tip)—that’s the needle side. Hold it like a marker, not a dart.
  • Inject It: Firmly press the needle end into their outer thigh (side of leg, midway between hip and knee). You can do it through jeans or shorts—no need to undress. Push hard until it clicks, then hold for 3 seconds (count “1-Mississippi, 2-Mississippi, 3-Mississippi”).
  • Remove and Massage: Pull it out straight (don’t twist), and rub the spot for 10 seconds to help the medicine spread. The needle retracts automatically in most pens—don’t reuse it.
  • Call 911 Right Away: Even if they feel better in a minute, dial emergency services or yell for someone to do it. Say, “Anaphylaxis, used an EpiPen, need help at [location].” The shot wears off in 10-20 minutes, and they need hospital care for a second wave or monitoring.
  • Stay with Them: Keep them calm, sitting up if breathing is hard, or lying down with legs raised if they’re dizzy. Watch for symptoms returning. If it gets worse and there’s a second EpiPen, use it after 5-15 minutes (check instructions).

What If There’s No EpiPen? If no injector is around, don’t wait—call 911 immediately. Keep them in a position that helps breathing (sitting up if wheezing, lying down if fainting). Don’t give food, drinks, or meds—it could block their airway more. Reassure them, like, “Help’s coming, hang in there,” and note the time symptoms started for medics. If breathing stops, start CPR (Section 2).

Teen Scenario Follow-Up: Back at the bake sale, Sam points to his backpack, wheezing, “Epi…Pen.” You grab it, pop the cap, jab his thigh for 3 seconds, and dial 911 on speaker. He breathes easier in a minute, but you stay by his side until paramedics roll in. Later, he’s like, “Dude, you saved my life!”—and you’re just glad you paid attention in class.

Prevention Tips for Teens: Got allergies yourself? Always carry your EpiPen and tell your crew where it is. At parties or cafeterias, snap pics of food labels or ask about ingredients—cross-contamination is sneaky. If your friend has allergies, be their backup: “Yo, is this safe for you?” can prevent a crisis. Start a group chat for your squad to share allergy alerts before hangouts.

Cool Fact for Teens: EpiPens are like sci-fi gadgets—some newer ones have voice instructions that talk you through it! And epinephrine? It’s basically adrenaline, the same stuff your body pumps during a jump-scare. You’re giving them a superhero boost!

Group Activity: Watch an EpiPen demo video online with friends (search “how to use EpiPen”—tons of quick clips). Use a marker or empty pen to “practice” the motion on an orange (thigh substitute). Quiz each other: “Cap off first or jab first?” Make it a meme-worthy moment but lock in the steps!
