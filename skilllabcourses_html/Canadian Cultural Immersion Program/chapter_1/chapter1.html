Chapter 1: Program Foundation and First-Week Preparation

 

Section 1: Canada at a Glance: Rights and Responsibilities for International Students

Overview

Canada is known for its multiculturalism, stunning landscapes, and high quality of life. As an international student, you’re about to become part of a diverse and welcoming community. However, with this opportunity comes a set of rights and responsibilities that will help you thrive while respecting Canadian laws and cultural norms.

Your Rights

As an international student in Canada, you are protected under various laws and policies. Here are some key rights:

  • Right to Education: You have the right to study at the institution listed on your study permit and access academic resources like libraries and tutoring.
  • Right to Fair Treatment: You’re protected against discrimination based on race, gender, religion, or nationality under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
  • Right to Work (Limited): Depending on your study permit, you may be eligible to work part-time on or off-campus (more on this in the next section).
  • Right to Healthcare: In most provinces, international students are eligible for public health insurance after a waiting period (e.g., in Ontario, it’s the Ontario Health Insurance Plan - OHIP, after 3 months). Until then, private insurance is mandatory.

Your Responsibilities

Alongside rights, you have responsibilities to uphold:

  • Comply with Study Permit Conditions: You must remain enrolled full-time, make academic progress, and not work more hours than permitted.
  • Respect Canadian Laws: This includes laws around drugs, alcohol (legal drinking age varies by province, e.g., 19 in Ontario, 18 in Alberta), and public behaviour.
  • Cultural Sensitivity: Canada values diversity, so be respectful of different cultures, languages (English and French are official languages), and customs.
  • Report Changes: Inform Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) if you change your address, school, or other key details.

Practical Example

Imagine you’ve just arrived in Toronto and notice a classmate struggling with language barriers. Instead of ignoring them, you could offer to help or connect them with campus language support services. This reflects cultural sensitivity and builds a supportive community—core Canadian values.

Activity: "Rights &amp; Responsibilities Role-Play"

  • Objective: Understand real-life scenarios where rights and responsibilities apply.
  • How It Works: Pair up with a friend or family member before arriving. Pretend one of you is an international student facing a challenge (e.g., a landlord refusing to return a deposit, or being asked to work overtime beyond permit limits). The other plays a campus advisor or friend offering advice based on the rights and responsibilities above.
  • Fun Element: Create a short skit and record it to share with peers in the program once you arrive. Add humour or props to make it engaging!

 

Section 2: Study Permit Basics and Work Eligibility Overview

Overview

Your study permit is your key to studying in Canada legally. Understanding its conditions and how it relates to work eligibility is crucial for staying compliant and making the most of your time here.

Study Permit Basics

  • What It Is: A document issued by IRCC that allows you to study at a specific Designated Learning Institution (DLI) in Canada.
  • Key Conditions: You must be enrolled full-time, report any changes to IRCC, and leave Canada or apply for an extension before your permit expires.
  • Duration: Typically valid for the length of your program plus 90 days, giving you time to decide your next steps (e.g., extend, apply for a post-graduation work permit, or leave).

Work Eligibility

  • On-Campus Work: If your study permit states you can work, you’re allowed to work on-campus without a separate work permit for an unlimited number of hours (though balancing studies is key).
  • Off-Campus Work: You can work up to 20 hours per week during regular academic sessions and full-time during scheduled breaks (like summer or winter holidays) if your permit allows it and you’re enrolled full-time at a DLI.
  • Co-op/Internship: If your program includes a mandatory work placement, you’ll need a co-op work permit in addition to your study permit.

Practical Example

Let’s say you’re studying at the University of British Columbia (UBC) and want to work as a barista at a local café. Check your study permit first—if it says “may work 20 hours per week off-campus,” you’re good to go during the semester, but only for 20 hours. During summer break, you can switch to full-time hours.

Activity: "Permit Puzzle Challenge"

  • Objective: Familiarize yourself with study permit rules and work eligibility.
  • How It Works: Create a list of 5 hypothetical scenarios (e.g., “I want to work 30 hours a week during exams” or “My program has a co-op term”). Research or review the rules on the IRCC website (or use this section) to decide if each scenario is allowed. Discuss with a friend or family member.
  • Fun Element: Turn it into a game—award points for correct answers and add a silly penalty for wrong ones, like singing a Canadian song like “O Canada”!

 

Section 3: Safety Net Resources: Campus and Community Support Systems

Overview

Canada offers a wide range of support systems to ensure you feel safe and supported. Knowing where to turn for help—whether it’s academic, emotional, or financial—can make a huge difference.

Campus Resources

  • International Student Offices: Most universities and colleges have dedicated offices to help with visas, cultural adjustment, and more (e.g., University of Toronto’s Centre for International Experience).
  • Counselling Services: Free mental health support for stress, anxiety, or homesickness.
  • Academic Support: Tutoring, writing centers, and libraries to help you succeed in your studies.
  • Student Unions: Join clubs or advocacy groups for social connections and support.

Community Resources

  • Settlement Services: Organizations like YMCA or local immigrant services offer free workshops on housing, job search, and language skills.
  • Public Libraries: Beyond books, they provide free internet, community events, and resources for newcomers.
  • Healthcare Clinics: Walk-in clinics or campus health centers for non-emergency medical needs.

Practical Example

If you’re feeling overwhelmed with assignments at McGill University, don’t hesitate to visit the Student Wellness Hub for counselling or academic workshops. If you’re off-campus in Montreal, a local settlement agency can help with finding affordable housing or language classes.

Activity: "Support System Scavenger Hunt"

  • Objective: Learn about resources before arrival.
  • How It Works: Research online the specific campus and community resources for the city or university you’ll be attending. Make a list of 3 campus and 3 community resources, including their contact info or website.
  • Fun Element: Share your findings in a creative format—like a treasure map drawing of where these “safety nets” are located on campus or in the city.

 

Section 4: First-Week Action Plan: Banking, Phone, Transit, Campus Offices

Overview

Your first week in Canada will be busy, but having a clear action plan will help you settle in quickly. Focus on these four priorities: banking, phone, transit, and campus offices.

Banking

  • Why: You’ll need a Canadian bank account for tuition payments, rent, and daily expenses.
  • How: Choose a major bank like RBC, TD, or Scotiabank, which often have student accounts with no fees. Bring your passport, study permit, and proof of address (like a lease agreement) to open an account.
  • Tip: Look for newcomer or student packages that include a debit card and sometimes a credit card with no annual fee.

Phone

  • Why: Staying connected is essential for safety and communication.
  • How: Get a prepaid or monthly plan from providers like Rogers, Bell, or Telus. Many offer student discounts. You’ll need ID and possibly a credit card.
  • Tip: Start with a cheap prepaid plan (around $10-20/month) until you decide on a long-term option.

Transit

  • Why: Public transit is the most affordable way to get around in most Canadian cities.
  • How: Research the transit system in your city (e.g., TTC in Toronto, TransLink in Vancouver). Buy a student pass if available—it’s often discounted. Download transit apps like Google Maps or Citymapper for routes.
  • Tip: Practice a route from your accommodation to campus before classes start.

Campus Offices

  • Why: Registering with key offices ensures you’re set for classes and support.
  • How: Visit the registrar’s office for course enrolment, the international student office for orientation, and the ID office for your student card.
  • Tip: Book appointments online if possible to avoid long waits.

Practical Example

On your second day in Vancouver, head to a nearby TD Bank with your documents to open a student account. Then, visit a Rogers store at a mall to get a prepaid SIM card for $15/month. Use the TransLink Compass Card to take a bus to Simon Fraser University and pick up your student ID.

Activity: "First-Week Simulation Game"

  • Objective: Prepare for the first week by simulating tasks.
  • How It Works: Create a checklist of the 4 tasks above. Pretend you’ve just arrived and “complete” each task by researching the specific bank, phone provider, transit system, and campus office for your city. Write a short journal entry about your “first day.”
  • Fun Element: Add a challenge—pretend one task goes wrong (e.g., forgot ID for banking). How would you solve it? Share your creative solutions with peers.

 

Section 5: Emergency Contacts and Crisis Resources

Overview

Emergencies can happen, but knowing who to call and where to go will keep you safe. Memorize these resources before you arrive.

Key Emergency Contacts

  • 911: For immediate emergencies (police, fire, medical). Only use for life-threatening situations.
  • Campus Security: Most campuses have 24/7 security numbers for on-campus incidents (check your university website).
  • Non-Emergency Police: For non-urgent issues like lost property or minor theft (varies by city, e.g., 416-808-2222 in Toronto).
  • Health Helplines: Provincial helplines for medical advice (e.g., Telehealth Ontario at 1-866-797-0000).

Crisis Resources

  • Mental Health Support: National crisis lines like Crisis Services Canada (1-833-456-4566) or Kids Help Phone (1-800-668-6868) for youth.
  • Shelters and Food Banks: Local organizations for housing or food insecurity (e.g., Daily Bread Food Bank in Toronto).
  • Embassy/Consulate: Contact your country’s embassy for passport issues or emergencies requiring repatriation.

Practical Example

If you’re alone late at night on campus in Calgary and feel unsafe, call campus security (not 911 unless it’s an immediate threat). If you’re struggling emotionally, reach out to a crisis helpline for confidential support instead of bottling it up.

Activity: "Emergency Contact Card Craft"

  • Objective: Have emergency info handy.
  • How It Works: Create a small card or note on your phone with key numbers (911, campus security, health helpline, embassy, a trusted friend/family). Include one crisis resource specific to your city.
  • Fun Element: Decorate the card with Canadian symbols (like a maple leaf) to make it personal. Keep it in your wallet or phone case.
